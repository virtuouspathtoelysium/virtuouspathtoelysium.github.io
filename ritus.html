1. THE VIRTUOUS PATH METHOD#

Applied section. Everything after §1 is evidence; §1 is practice. The two are kept apart so that no adjustment made here can ever be mistaken for an attested ancient act.

1.0 How this section is built#

Every rite below is set out first as a plain procedure, then backed by the ancient sources it rests on. Each step carries one of three marks.

Roman rite The step is performed as Rome performed it. Where both Rome and Greece attest a rite and neither offends a virtue, Rome is followed.

Greek rite The step is performed as Greece performed it, because Rome has no attested rite for it. Greek acts are adopted whole, under Roman names where a counterpart exists (Hestia becomes Vesta).

Virtuous Both Rome and Greece did something here that conflicts with one or more of the twenty-one virtues. The ancient act is changed or dropped, the virtue is named, and wherever possible the replacement is itself an attested ancient practice. Nothing is drawn from any tradition outside Greece and Rome.

Where a step is unchanged it is simply performed. Where the ancient sources give the words, the words are given below the procedure in the original language with a plain English rendering. Every ruling made under these marks is logged at §1.8.

1.1 The offering — what is given, and how it is given#

This path offers no animal. That single change runs through every rite in this section, so both halves of it are set out here once: first what is given in place of the victim, then the order in which any formal offering is made. The daily household rites at §1.2 are the short, domestic form of the same act; everything below applies to them as well.

1.1.1 What is given in place of the victim#

This path offers no animal. This is the single change that runs through every rite below, so it is set out once here. It is not a modern invention: the ancient world had a complete bloodless offering vocabulary and used it constantly.

The procedure

  1. VirtuousIn place of the animal victim, offer a cake — a popanum or libum — baked, where the rite names an animal, in the shape of that animal.Compassion. The cake is itself an attested ancient substitute (Diasia; Zeus Hypatos).
  2. VirtuousIn place of the blood on the altar, pour wine at the same place and moment.Compassion. Cato's daps is a cup of wine and nothing more.
  3. VirtuousIn place of the entrails offered on the flame, place first-fruits — grain, fruit, honeycomb, oil — on the flame in the same act.Compassion. The Phigalian rite of Demeter offered exactly this.
  4. VirtuousIn place of reading the entrails for acceptance, watch the flame when the offering is placed: a clean, bright taking of the offering is the sign.Truth and Compassion. The flare of the household fire was itself a Roman omen.
  5. Roman riteEverything else stays as Rome had it: salted meal (mola salsa) sprinkled on the offering; milk, honey, oil, wine, incense, flowers, garlands, eggs, wool — none of which requires a death.
  6. VirtuousWhere the ancient rite used hide, leather, or thongs from a killed animal, use wool, linen or plant fibre in the same place.Compassion. Wool is itself among Ovid's named februa.

The ancient sources for this procedure

  • Ovid, Fasti 1.337–338 Tier DAnte, deos homini quod conciliare valeret, / far erat et puri lucida mica salis.In earlier days, what had power to reconcile the gods to man was spelt, and a gleaming grain of pure salt.Steps 1, 5 — the original Roman offering was bloodless; Ovid goes on (1.349–456) to narrate animal sacrifice as the later form.
  • Thucydides 1.126.6 (Diasia) Tier B…ἐν ᾗ πανδημεὶ θύουσι πολλοὶ οὐχ ἱερεῖα ἀλλὰ θύματα ἐπιχώρια.…at which the whole people sacrifice, many of them offering not animal victims but local offerings. (The scholiast identifies these as popana, cakes in the shape of animals.)Step 1 — a bloodless substitute accepted at a major state festival, by a large share of the citizen body.
  • Pausanias 1.26.5 (altar of Zeus Hypatos, Athens) Tier B…ἐφ' οὗ ζῷον θύουσιν οὐδέν, πέμματα δὲ θέντες οὐδὲν ἔτι οἴνῳ χρήσασθαι νομίζουσι.…on which they sacrifice nothing living, but lay cakes upon it, and it is not their custom to use wine.Step 1 — a permanently bloodless altar in the most public sanctuary in Greece.
  • Pausanias 8.42.11 (Demeter Melaina, Phigalia) Tier B(Greek prose; rendered.)They offer fruits of cultivated trees, especially grapes, and honeycombs, and unspun wool still full of its grease; these they place on the altar and pour olive oil over them.Steps 3, 5 — a complete bloodless offering formula from a working sanctuary.
  • Cato, De Agricultura 132 Tier A…culignam vini dapi……a cup of wine as the feast-offering…Step 2 — a cup of wine is, by Cato's instruction, a complete rite to Jupiter.
  • Servius, ad Aeneidem 1.730 Tier C(Latin commentary; rendered.)At the second course, silence was kept while a portion was placed in the fire, and the household observed whether the flame flared.Step 4 — the flare of the fire was already a Roman omen, independent of any victim.
  • Censorinus, De Die Natali 2.2–3 Tier C(Latin; rendered.)Some hold that a twofold offering should be made to the Genius on a birthday, of wine and flowers only; the reason given is that it would be wrong to take away life on the day one received one's own.Steps 1–5 — an explicitly bloodless rule attached to a Roman cult.
  • Ovid, Fasti 2.19–28 (the februa) Tier Dfebrua Romani dixere piamina patres…Our Roman fathers called instruments of purification februa… (the februa named are pieces of wool, pine branches, and roasted spelt with salt).Step 6 — wool, pine and salted spelt are attested purifiers; no hide is needed.

A note on scale. Do not compensate for the absence of a victim by inflating the offering. Cato's pro copia — "according to her means" — and Horace's grain of salt are the rule. A cup of wine offered correctly is a complete rite.

1.1.2 The order of a formal offering#

The Roman sequence, kept in full, with the animal replaced. Use it for any formal offering: a festival, a vow, a request.

The procedure

  1. VirtuousSet the offering table. On the ara or a clean cloth place the cake (popanum or libum), the wine, the incense, the salted meal (mola salsa), and flowers.Compassion. The cake stands where the victim was led.
  2. Roman riteCommand silence — favete linguis.
  3. Roman riteWash the hands.
  4. Roman riteVeil the head; face east.
  5. Roman riteOpening offering (praefatio): a grain of incense and a little wine on the flame, before the god is named.
  6. Roman riteRaise the hands, palms upturned (manibus supinis; palms down, toward the earth, for a god of the dead or the underworld), then name the god and the epithet; add the safeguard clause if the addressee is uncertain — si deus, si dea es.
  7. VirtuousSprinkle the salted meal on the cake, as it was once sprinkled on the victim's back.Same act, new recipient.
  8. VirtuousPour wine over the cake, where the victim's forehead would have been wetted.Replaces the blood.
  9. VirtuousPlace the cake and the first-fruits on the flame.Replaces the offering of the entrails.
  10. VirtuousWatch the flame. If it takes the offering cleanly, the rite is accepted.Replaces the reading of the entrails; the flare of the fire is an attested Roman omen.
  11. Roman riteIf the flame gutters or refuses, begin again from step 2 with a fresh offering.
  12. Roman riteIf any word was fumbled, speak the repair clause (below) and continue.
  13. Roman riteTurn a complete circle to the right, imitating the revolution of the heavens, and unveil.
  14. Roman riteThe household eats what remains.

The ancient sources for this procedure

  • Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities 7.72 Tier B(Greek prose; rendered.)An eyewitness account of the Roman procession and sacrifice at the Ludi Romani: the victims led, the hands washed, the incense and wine offered first, the salted meal, the veiled officiant.Steps 2–5 — the order of the Roman rite.
  • Vergil, Aeneid 3.405–407 Tier Dpurpureo velare comas adopertus amictu…Veil your hair, covered with a purple robe…Step 4
  • Vitruvius, De Architectura 4.5.1 Tier B(Latin; rendered.)The temple and its image face west, so that the worshipper at the altar looks east toward the image and the rising sun.Step 4 — facing east.
  • Cato, De Agricultura 139 Tier ASi deus, si dea es, quoium illud sacrum est, uti tibi ius est porco piaculo facere illiusce sacri coercendi ergo… bonas preces precor, uti sies volens propitius mihi domo familiaeque meae liberisque meis.Whether you are a god or a goddess to whom this place is sacred… I make good prayers to you, that you may be willing and favourable to me, to my house and household, and to my children.Step 6 — the safeguard clause, and the standard request formula 'willing and favourable'.
  • Servius, ad Eclogas 8.82 Tier C(Latin; rendered.)The mola salsa is toasted emmer, ground and mixed with salt, made by the Vestals from the first ears of the harvest; the verb immolare means to apply the meal.Step 7 — the salted meal.
  • Cato, De Agricultura 141 — the repair clause Tier AMars pater, si quid tibi in illisce suovitaurilibus lactentibus neque satisfactum est, te hisce suovitaurilibus piaculo.Father Mars, if anything in that earlier offering was not satisfactory to you, I make expiation to you with this one.Step 12 — the formula for correcting a fumbled rite. Substitute the god's name.
  • Pliny, Naturalis Historia 28.11 Tier B…quo si quid praetereatur aut praeposterum dicatur……if any word is omitted or spoken out of order, the whole rite is void and must be begun again.Step 11
  • Vergil, Aeneid 3.176–177 Tier D…tendoque supinas / ad caelum cum voce manus……and I stretch my upturned hands to heaven with a cry…Step 6 — the upturned palms.
  • Homer, Iliad 1.450 Tier B…μεγάλ' εὔχετο χεῖρας ἀνασχών……he prayed aloud, lifting up his hands…Step 6 — the raised hands, in the Greek form.
  • Pliny, Naturalis Historia 28.25 Tier B…totumque corpus circumagimus.…and we turn the whole body around.Step 13
  • Plutarch, Life of Numa 14 Tier C(Greek; rendered.)Why do they turn themselves round in a circle when they worship? … Is it an imitation of the revolution of the universe?Step 13 — the turn is a full rotation, not a quarter turn.

The exactitude is kept in full and is not optional. Exact words, exact order, total attention. It answers to Diligence, Mindfulness, Discipline and Piety.

1.2 The daily household rite#

Roman at its core; Greece supplies the evening observance, which Rome lacks. A word on terms: the lararium is the household shrine — a niche, a painted panel, or a small columned cabinet (aedicula) holding the household gods; the focus is the hearth-fire, which is Vesta herself and needs no image; the ara is the altar-stand before the shrine on which the flame and the offerings are set — in a small home, a fireproof dish or a standing lamp serves. The Lares guard the household, the Penates the store-cupboard, and the Genius is the indwelling spirit of the head of the house.

1.2.1 Morning#

On rising, before the day's work. The whole rite takes a few minutes.

The procedure

  1. Roman riteWash. Draw fresh water and rinse both hands; touch the wet hand to the face. Do not approach the shrine with unwashed hands.
  2. Roman riteGo to the lararium — the household shrine. Open its doors or draw back its cloth. Stand; one does not sit before the gods of the house.
  3. Roman riteVeil the head. Draw a fold of the toga, a mantle, or a clean cloth up over the crown of the head so that it covers you from behind and frames the face.
  4. Roman riteLight the lamp on the ara, the altar-stand before the shrine. If a lamp has burned through the night, trim and feed it instead.
  5. Roman riteMake the offering. Lay a few grains of incense on the flame, or pour a little wine into the dish on the ara, or set a fresh garland on the shrine. Any one is enough; all three is the full daily form.
  6. Roman ritePray aloud, standing, with the hands raised — manibus supinis, the arms lifted and the palms turned upward toward the sky, or toward the cult image if one stands in the shrine. This is the posture for the gods above, and it is not optional: ancient prayer was made with the body as much as the voice. For a god of the underworld or of the dead — the Manes, Dis, Ceres in her chthonic aspect — the palms are turned downward toward the earth instead, and the ground may be struck with the hand. Then speak, in a fixed order kept the same every day: name the god; name the epithet; state the request; state what you are offering; close. If unsure of the right name, add: "or by whatever name it is right to call you."
  7. Roman riteTurn the whole body in a complete circle to the right — a full rotation, not merely a quarter turn to face right — then unveil. The turn imitates the revolution of the heavens and closes the sacred space about you. The morning rite is complete.

The ancient sources for this procedure

  • Persius, Satires 2.15–16 Tier B…Tiberino in gurgite mergis / mane caput bis terque et noctem flumine purgas.…you plunge your head in Tiber's stream two or three times in the morning, and wash away the night with the river.Step 1
  • Hesiod, Works and Days 724–726 Tier Bμηδέ ποτ' ἐξ ἠοῦς Διὶ λείβειν αἴθοπα οἶνον / χερσὶν ἀνίπτοισιν…Never at dawn pour gleaming wine to Zeus with unwashed hands… (the gods do not hear, and spit the prayers back).Step 1
  • Vergil, Aeneid 3.405–407 Tier Dpurpureo velare comas adopertus amictu, / ne qua inter sanctos ignis in honore deorum / hostilis facies occurrat et omina turbet.Veil your hair, covered with a purple robe, so that no hostile face may intrude among the holy fires in the worship of the gods and disturb the omens.Step 3 — the veiled head; confirmed by the veiled Genius on almost every Pompeian shrine.
  • Plautus, Aulularia, prologue 23–25 Tier B…quod mihi cottidie / aut ture aut vino aut aliqui semper supplicat, / dat mihi coronas.…because she makes supplication to me every single day, with incense, or wine, or something, and gives me garlands.Step 5 — the household Lar names the daily offering himself: incense, wine, garland.
  • Pliny, Naturalis Historia 28.11 Tier B…de scripto praeire aliquem rursusque alium custodem dari qui adtendat, alium vero praeponi qui favere linguis iubeat……that someone reads out the formula in advance from a script, that another is set as a guardian to attend to it, and another put over them to command sacred silence…Step 6 — the words are exact and audible; a fumbled rite is begun again.
  • Homer, Iliad 1.450 Tier Bτοῖσιν δὲ Χρύσης μεγάλ' εὔχετο χεῖρας ἀνασχών…And Chryses prayed aloud for them, lifting up his hands…Step 6 — the raised hands. The formula χεῖρας ἀνασχών, "having lifted up the hands," is the standing Homeric description of prayer.
  • Vergil, Aeneid 3.176–177 Tier Dcorripio e stratis corpus tendoque supinas / ad caelum cum voce manus…I snatch my body from the bed and stretch my upturned hands to heaven with a cry…Step 6 — supinas manus, the upturned palms, named outright. Compare Aeneid 4.205.
  • Homer, Iliad 9.568–570 Tier B…χερσὶ δὲ γαῖαν / πολλὰ ῥοῦσα κικλήσκουσ' Ἀΐδην καὶ ἐπαινὴν Περσεφόνειαν……beating the earth with her hands, she called upon Hades and dread Persephone…Step 6 — the inverted posture for the powers below: the hands go down to the earth, not up to the sky.
  • Pliny, Naturalis Historia 28.25 Tier Bin adorando dexteram ad osculum referimus totumque corpus circumagimus.In worship we bring the right hand to the lips, and turn the whole body around.Step 7 — totum corpus circumagimus, "we turn the whole body around."
  • Plutarch, Life of Numa 14 Tier C(Greek; rendered.)Why do worshippers turn themselves round in a circle? … Or is the turning an imitation of the revolution of the universe? … Rather, the man who has adored the god turns round with him, completing the circle.Step 7 — the decisive text: the act is a complete rotation, not a turn to the right hand. Plutarch offers several explanations and settles on the imitation of the turning heavens.
  • Cato, De Agricultura 139 Tier ASi deus, si dea es, quoium illud sacrum est…Whether you are a god or a goddess to whom this place is sacred…Step 6 — the safeguard clause where the addressee is uncertain.

The shortest valid form: wash the hands, veil the head, lay a grain of incense on the lamp-flame, and speak the god's name with a word of greeting.

1.2.2 At the table#

At the main meal of the day. The household gods share the table.

The procedure

  1. Roman riteAs the main course is served, call for silence — favete linguis, "keep your tongues favourable," that is, let no ill-omened word be spoken.
  2. Roman riteTake a portion of the food from the table.
  3. Roman ritePlace it on the hearth-flame — the focus — or, where the table is away from the hearth, in the dish before the lararium, with a word to the Lares.
  4. VirtuousWatch — and listen to — the flame. If it takes the offering cleanly and brightens, the offering is received. Listen for the salt. Grains of mola salsa thrown on a hot flame crackle, spit and leap; the ancients heard that lively snapping as the god taking the food with pleasure, and Horace calls it the leaping grain of salt. A silent, sullen flame that smothers the offering is the contrary sign.Truth and Compassion. Rome read the entrails of a victim for this sign; the flare of the household fire is itself an attested Roman omen and takes over the office.
  5. Roman riteResume the meal. What the flame does not consume, the household eats. It is not thrown away.

The ancient sources for this procedure

  • Pliny, Naturalis Historia 28.26–27 Tier B(Latin; rendered.)Silence is kept at table for religious reasons, and the formula favete linguis is spoken.Step 1
  • Servius, ad Aeneidem 1.730 Tier C…silentium fieri solebat, quoad ea quae de cena libata fuerant ad focum ferrentur et in ignem darentur, ac puer deos propitios nuntiasset.…silence used to be kept until what had been taken from the dinner as an offering was carried to the hearth and put into the fire, and a boy had announced that the gods were favourable.Steps 2–4 — the portion carried to the hearth, and the sign read from the fire.
  • Homeric Hymn 29, To Hestia, 4–6 Tier Bοὐ γὰρ ἄτερ σοῦ εἰλαπίναι θνητοῖσιν, ἵν' οὐ πρώτῃ πυμάτῃ τε Ἑστίῃ ἀρχόμενος σπένδει μελιηδέα οἶνον.For without you there are no feasts among mortals, where one does not, beginning and ending, pour honey-sweet wine to Hestia.Step 3 — the hearth receives its share at every meal.
  • Horace, Odes 3.23.17–20 Tier Bimmunis aram si tetigit manus, / non sumptuosa blandior hostia / mollivit aversos Penatis / farre pio et saliente mica.If a guiltless hand has touched the altar, it has softened the Penates' turned-away faces no less winningly than a costly victim — with pious spelt and a leaping grain of salt.Step 4 — saliente mica, the leaping grain of salt: the crackle of salt in the flame, and the statement that a small clean offering is as acceptable as a costly one.

Where there is no hearth at the table: set the portion in a small dish before the lararium and pour a little wine to the Lares.

1.2.3 Evening#

At nightfall, or as the household settles. Rome has no attested evening household rite of its own; Greece states one plainly, and it is followed here at the Roman hearth.

A ruling on the head. Greece supplies the cadence of this rite — that there is an evening pouring at all — but not its manner. The ritus Graecus, in which a Roman worships bareheaded, is itself a Roman prescription attached to two named cults, Saturn and Hercules at the Great Altar; it does not attach to every act whose written source happens to be Greek. This rite is performed at a Roman hearth, to Vesta, by a Roman household. Therefore the head is veiled, morning and evening alike. The single exception is the one Rome itself made: if the evening prayer is addressed to Saturn or to Hercules, uncover.

The procedure

  1. Roman riteReturn to the hearth or the lararium and veil the head as in the morning. The veil is kept even though the pouring that follows is Greek in origin — see the ruling above. Uncover only if the prayer is to Saturn or to Hercules.
  2. Greek ritePour the evening libation — a little wine onto the hearth or into the dish; where a wineless offering is wanted, milk or clean water.
  3. Greek riteGive the first pouring and the last to Vesta, whatever other god the rite addresses. She opens and closes every domestic observance.
  4. Roman riteSpeak the evening prayer with the hands raised and the palms upturned, in the same fixed order as the morning; then turn a complete circle to the right and unveil.
  5. Roman riteBank the hearth — cover the embers so the fire holds through the night — and put out the lamp unless the household keeps one burning until dawn. This is housekeeping, not rite; no ancient text governs it.

The ancient sources for this procedure

  • Hesiod, Works and Days 338–339 Tier Bἄλλοτε δὲ σπονδῇσι θύεσσί τε ἱλάσκεσθαι, / ἠμὲν ὅτ' εὐνάζῃ καὶ ὅτ' ἂν φάος ἱερὸν ἔλθῃ.And at other times propitiate them with libations and burnt offerings, both when you go to bed and when the holy light returns.Step 2 — the only explicit ancient statement of a twice-daily household libation.
  • Homeric Hymn 29, To Hestia, 4–6 Tier B…πρώτῃ πυμάτῃ τε Ἑστίῃ ἀρχόμενος σπένδει μελιηδέα οἶνον.…beginning and ending, one pours honey-sweet wine to Hestia.Step 3 — first and last to the hearth.
  • Cato, De Agricultura 132 Tier Asi voles, Vestae dato.If you wish, make an offering to Vesta.Step 3 — the Roman habit of attaching Vesta to any rite.
  • Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.8.2 and 3.6.17; Servius, ad Aeneidem 3.407 Tier C(Latin; rendered.)At the altar of Saturn, and at the Great Altar of Hercules, the rite is performed by the Greek usage, with the head uncovered; Servius distinguishes the ritus Graecus from the ritus Romanus outright.Step 1 — the basis of the ruling above. The bareheaded usage is a Roman rule for two named cults, not a rule for anything of Greek origin.

The one rule to remember: the day's first pouring and its last both go to Vesta at the hearth.

Two standing prohibitions. No blood, at any point, ever (§1.1). No offering to any human being, living or dead, as to a god: the Manes of the household's virtuous dead are honoured — named, garlanded, fed at the proper seasons — and never addressed as gods.

1.3 The monthly and annual household cadence#

What is owed, and when. Cato gives the floor; Plautus gives the ceiling.

The procedure

  1. Roman riteOn the Kalends, the Nones and the Ides of every month, and on festival days: put a garland on the hearth and make supplication to the Lar of the household. This is the minimum obligation.
  2. Roman riteDaily, as devotion rather than obligation: incense, wine, or a garland at the shrine (§1.2).
  3. Roman riteThe Kalends belong to Juno; the Ides to Jupiter. Name them on their days.
  4. Roman riteOn the birthday of the head of the household: offer to the Genius — wine, incense, flowers, cakes. Never blood.
  5. Greek riteKeep a jar in the store-cupboard. Set a small lidded jar there, wind its handles with white wool, and fill it with pure water, olive oil, and a mixture of every kind of produce in the house. Renew it as the stores turn over. Address it to the Penates.
  6. Roman riteAt the Compitalia (movable, midwinter): hang one woollen figure at the crossroads shrine for every member of the household.Justice. Rome hung figures for the free and balls for the enslaved; one figure for every person

The ancient sources for this procedure

  • Cato, De Agricultura 143 Tier AKalendis, Idibus, Nonis, festus dies cum erit, coronam in focum indat, per eosdemque dies lari familiari pro copia supplicet.On the Kalends, the Ides, the Nones, and when there is a feast day, let her put a garland on the hearth, and on those same days let her make supplication to the Lar of the household according to her means.Step 1 — the only surviving ancient rule for how often the household must observe.
  • Plautus, Aulularia, prologue 23–25 Tier B…quod mihi cottidie… supplicat……because she makes supplication to me every single day…Step 2 — daily devotion is attested as the exceptional, honoured case.
  • Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.15.16–19 Tier C(Latin; rendered.)The Kalends are Juno's; the Ides are Jupiter's, on which the sheep of the Ides is offered by the Flamen Dialis.Step 3

1.4 Births#

Roman throughout, with one Greek act where Rome has none, and two changes on grounds of virtue. Days are counted from the birth.

The procedure

  1. Roman riteNight of the birth — invoke Juno Lucina. Loosen all knots, belts and bindings in the room, and unbind the hair: bound things are held to bind the birth.
  2. VirtuousKeep vigil through the night and keep the hearth lit. The old rite of three men striking the threshold with axe, pestle and broom against Silvanus is not performed.Truth. Performing an apotropaic act one does not credit is a falsehood in action. The vigil and the lit hearth remain.
  3. Greek riteFifth day — carry the child at a walk around the household hearth, in the presence of those who attended the birth.Rome has no such act; Greece does (the Amphidromia). The Greek rite ran, and in its original setting decided whether the child was reared. Here it is walked and decides nothing: the child is already received.
  4. Roman riteEighth day for a girl, ninth for a boy — the dies lustricus: make an offering, give the name, and place the amulet (bulla).
  5. VirtuousThe amulet is a sign of the child's standing and the family's obligation, worn until adulthood and then dedicated at the shrine. No claim is made that it protects.Truth.
  6. VirtuousPresent the child before assembled kin and fellow worshippers, and write down the names of those present.Rome presented the child to the curia and Greece to the phratry; neither body exists. The witnesses were the record; the function is kept.

1.5 Weddings#

A wedding is a three-day affair. Day One is the Proaulia, the leave-taking; Day Two the Gamos, the wedding proper; Day Three the Epaulia, the settling-in. Roman throughout. Most of the rite is kept exactly; one Greek act is used where Rome has none, and four steps are changed on grounds of virtue.

Day One — Proaulia (the day before)

The bride takes leave of her girlhood: her childhood things dedicated, offerings made to the gods of marriage, and the ritual bath.

  1. Greek riteDedicate the bride's childhood things — her toys and her girlhood dress — at the household shrine, and make the offerings to the gods of marriage. Then the nuptial bath, the water fetched by a young relative from a named running source.Rome has no attested nuptial bath; Athens does.

Day Two — Gamos (the wedding day)

The dressing, the binding of the match, the consent, the torchlit procession, and the entry into the new house.

  1. Roman riteDress the bride: the one-piece tunica recta; the woollen belt tied in the Hercules knot, which only the husband may untie; the flame-coloured veil (flammeum); a wreath of verbena and marjoram she gathered herself.
  2. VirtuousPart the hair into six locks (seni crines) with a plain bronze or bone pin.Compassion and Respect. Rome preferred a spear-point drawn from the body of a killed gladiator; the act of parting is what is attested, the implement's provenance is a separate custom, and a marriage does not begin with an object whose value came from a killing.
  3. Roman riteTake the auspices. A woman married only once (pronuba) joins the couple's right hands. Seal the contract before witnesses. Share the spelt cake — the oldest form, and already bloodless.
  4. Roman riteThe bride speaks the consent: Ubi tu Gaius, ego Gaia — "Where you are Gaius, I am Gaia."
  5. VirtuousThe bride steps out to her escort, having spoken her consent.Truth, Respect, Concord. Rome staged a seizure of the bride from her mother's arms; a staged abduction contradicts the consent given in the same hour.
  6. VirtuousTorchlit procession to the groom's house — five torches of whitethorn, three boys with both parents living, one before her with the torch and two leading her by the hand — with a hymn of praise sung along the way.Purity of speech. Rome sang obscene verses (the Fescennina) at the couple; Greece sang the hymenaios. Where both traditions are attested and one is plainly the more virtuous, the more virtuous is followed: the Greek hymn replaces the Roman jest.
  7. Roman riteThrow nuts to the children.
  8. VirtuousAt the door, the bride winds wool about the doorposts and anoints them with olive oil.Compassion. Rome used wolf's fat; oil is the standard ancient anointing substance.
  9. Roman riteShe is carried over the threshold, never touching it, and is given fire and water — the two things that make her of the household.

Day Three — Epaulia (the day after)

The couple are visited and provisioned, and the bride takes up her office at her own hearth.

  1. Roman riteThe repotia: a second dinner, and the bride's first offering as mistress of the house at the new hearth.

1.6 Funerals#

Roman throughout, framed by Cicero's account of the funerary law and Polybius' eyewitness account of the procession. One Greek act where Rome has none; two changes on grounds of virtue.

The procedure

  1. Roman riteAt the moment of death, the nearest kin catches the last breath. Then the conclamatio: call the dead by name aloud, at intervals. Close the eyes.
  2. Roman riteWash the body with warm water and anoint it.
  3. Roman riteLay out the body in the atrium on the funeral couch, feet toward the door, and set cypress at the entrance as the sign of a house of death.
  4. Greek riteSet a basin of water at the door, fetched from outside the house; everyone who leaves washes from it.Rome sprinkled the mourners at the end; Athens kept a standing vessel at the door throughout. Rome has no equivalent; the Greek object fills the gap.
  5. VirtuousOffer grain, salt and wine to Ceres in the presence of the body, to release the household from mourning.Compassion. Rome killed a sow (the porca praesentanea) for this; Ceres receives bloodless gifts in Roman sources already.
  6. Roman riteProcession to the grave outside the settlement, the ancestor masks carried by kin, torches before.
  7. VirtuousThe family's own lament, sung or spoken, and the eulogy.Truth and Respect. Rome hired women to wail and a mime to impersonate the dead man's voice and gait; purchased grief is performed grief, and impersonating the dead for the mourners is a falsehood about a person who cannot answer.
  8. Roman riteBurial or cremation. If cremating: the nearest kin lights the pyre with face turned away; spices and gifts are cast on; the fire is quenched with wine; the bones gathered, washed, and placed in the urn.
  9. Roman riteSweep the house with a broom, the heir performing it. Hold the funeral meal at the grave.
  10. Roman riteOn the ninth day, the closing offering at the tomb and the ninth-day dinner.
  11. Roman riteEvery February 13–21, the Parentalia: a tile wreathed with garlands, scattered grain, a grain of salt, bread soaked in wine, and loose violets, set at the tomb.

1.7 Festival adjustments#

Only festivals that require a change are listed; everything else in §8 is performed as attested, subject always to §1.1. The full step-by-step rites are on the Festivals page.

The procedure

  1. VirtuousCompitalia — one woollen figure for every member of the household, without distinction of status.Justice, Concord. Rome hung figures for the free and balls for the enslaved.
  2. Roman riteParentalia — performed exactly as Ovid gives it: tile, garlands, grain, salt, wine-soaked bread, violets. The model rite of this path.
  3. VirtuousFeralia — the tomb offering is kept; the rite to Tacita (lead, thread, beans in the mouth, a sewn fish-head, words binding hostile tongues) is not performed.Truth, Benevolence. It is binding magic against named people.
  4. VirtuousCaristia — the family meal, incense to the household gods and food before the Lares are kept exactly; the toast to the Genius of the emperor becomes a libation to the Genius of the household and to the family's own dead.Truth, Piety. No human being is addressed as a god.
  5. VirtuousLupercalia — the killings, the blood on the foreheads, the run and the striking of bystanders are not performed. The substitution comes from the same chapter of the same author.
  6. VirtuousFordicidia — not performed; the whole rite is the killing of pregnant cows. Offer Tellus first-fruits, grain and milk on the day instead.Compassion.
  7. VirtuousParilia — performed in full, with one change: the fumigant is made from hearth-ash, bean-straw, and the ash of the olive, pine, savin and laurel that the rite burns in any case, instead of calf-ash and horse-blood.Compassion.
  8. VirtuousRobigalia — the prayer is kept entire; incense, wine and a cake replace the entrails of a dog and a sheep.Compassion.
  9. Roman riteLemuria — performed in full and unchanged, including the fica gesture, which is directed at shades and engages no virtue.
  10. VirtuousVestalia — the salted meal is made by the household, since there is no Vestal order: emmer toasted, ground and mixed with salt, prepared in purity and used for nothing else.An adjustment for the absence of an institution, not for virtue.

2. How to read this document#

2.1 The central problem#

No ancient author wrote a manual of worship for the layman. Greek and Roman religion was transmitted by apprenticeship and by leges sacrae (sacred laws) carved on stone for individual sanctuaries, not by scripture. What survives is: one genuine procedural handbook (Cato); poetry describing ritual; comedy parodying it; antiquarian reportage; forensic prose where a lawsuit turned on whether a rite was done correctly; and the physical remains of the rites themselves.

CAntiquarian report by a later author quoting earlier ones. Plutarch, Festus, Macrobius, Servius, Athenaeus, scholia. Usable; flag the chain.

On Olynthos and Priene (Tier M): these two sites are the evidential backbone for Greek house-cult, because both were destroyed and abandoned (Olynthos 348 BCE; Priene rebuilt on a grid) and so preserve ordinary domestic plans rather than palaces. Fixed hearths, courtyard altar bases, and in-house votive deposits are recorded there. They confirm the locations the texts assume without ever describing.

3.2 THE DAY — Evening (the Greek day begins at sunset)#

Step 1. Evening libation (spondē, a pouring of liquid offering).

ἄλλοτε δὲ σπονδῇσι θύεσσί τε ἱλάσκεσθαι, / ἠμὲν ὅτ' εὐνάζῃ καὶ ὅτ' ἂν φάος ἱερὸν ἔλθῃ "And at other times propitiate them with libations and burnt offerings, both when you go to bed and when the holy light returns."

— Hesiod, Works and Days 338–339 (Tier B). This is the only explicit ancient statement of a twice-daily Greek household libation schedule — at bedtime and at dawn. Everything else about daily cadence is inference from it.

Step 2. First and last pouring goes to the hearth.

οὐ γὰρ ἄτερ σοῦ εἰλαπίναι θνητοῖσιν, ἵν' οὐ πρώτῃ πυμάτῃ τε Ἑστίῃ ἀρχόμενος σπένδει μελιηδέα οἶνον "For without you there are no feasts among mortals, where one does not, beginning and ending, pour honey-sweet wine to Hestia."

— Homeric Hymn 29, To Hestia, 4–6 (Tier B). Practical consequence: any domestic rite opens and closes at the hearth (hestia), whichever god is its object.

Step 3. The three libations at wine-drinking. The mixing-bowl (kratēr) is crowned; three pourings follow — to Zeus and the Olympians, to the Heroes, and to Zeus Sōtēr (the Savior) third. — Aeschylus, Agamemnon 245–247 (Tier B); Athenaeus 2.38c–d and 15.692f–693a (Tier C, collecting earlier comic poets). M: the kratēr, oinochoē (wine-jug) and phialē (shallow libation dish) are the standard symposium assemblage, and the phialē appears in the hand of gods and mortals on hundreds of vases — a pouring vessel with no foot, which cannot be set down full, i.e. designed for a single act of pouring.

3.3 THE DAY — Morning#

Step 4. Wash the hands before any prayer or pouring.

μηδέ ποτ' ἐξ ἠοῦς Διὶ λείβειν αἴθοπα οἶνον / χερσὶν ἀνίπτοισιν "Never at dawn pour gleaming wine to Zeus with unwashed hands."

— Hesiod, Works and Days 724–726 (Tier B), which continues that the gods do not hear and spit the prayers back. Compare 737–741 on crossing rivers unwashed.

Purifying water is chernips (χέρνιψ, lustral water). M: the sanctuary-scale version is the perirrhantērion (περιρραντήριον, a stone basin on a stand) — dozens survive in situ at the entrances of Greek sanctuaries, including at Isthmia and Olympia. Their placement at thresholds is the physical proof that purification is a boundary rite, performed on entering sacred ground.

Step 5. Morning libation. Per Hesiod, Works and Days 338–339 above (Tier B) — "when the holy light returns."

Step 6. On going out: the door and crossroads cults. The single richest text for everyday Greek household ritual is a satire, which is why it itemizes:

"If a weasel crosses his path he will not go on until someone else has passed, or until he has thrown three stones over the road... At the sight of the smooth stones at the crossroads he pours oil on them from his flask, and falls on his knees and makes obeisance before he goes away... he crowns the Herms."

— Theophrastus, Characters 16, On Superstition (Tier B). My rendering; read the whole chapter, it is two pages. Also in it: bay leaf in the mouth on going out; sprinkling from a sacred spring; summoning priestesses to purify him with a squill (skilla, sea-onion) or a puppy; monthly initiation with the Orphic priests.

Because it is parody, each individual act is real and recognizable; performing all of them is the joke. Treat the items as attested, not the density.

M for this step: oil-stained and worn stones at crossroads do not survive identifiably, but the hekataia (small triple Hekate statues) from the Athenian Agora and Delos, and the herms, do — and their findspots are doorways and street corners, exactly where Theophrastus puts the behaviour.

3.4 THE DAY — Meals and the storeroom#

Step 7. Zeus Ktesios (Zeus of Property) — the jar in the storeroom. The best-documented single piece of Greek household cult, because an exēgētēs (professional expounder of sacred law) wrote the instruction down:

"Set up the kadiskos (small jar) with a fresh lid; wreathe its handles with white wool; and pour in ambrosia. And ambrosia is: pure water, olive oil, and pankarpia (all-fruits — a mixture of every kind of produce). Pour these in."

— Anticlides, Exēgētikos, quoted at Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 11.473b–c (Tier A content inside a Tier C container). This is the only surviving verbatim Greek instruction for setting up a household cult object.

Its legal weight:

"He took me to the sacrifice to Zeus Ktesios, which he took most seriously; he brought neither slaves nor free men outside the family to it, but did everything himself."

— Isaeus 8.16 (On the Estate of Ciron) (Tier A, my rendering). The speaker uses admission to this rite as courtroom proof of kinship. That single argument is the strongest evidence that this was a real, regular, family-restricted rite.

M: household storage magazines with sunken pithoi (large storage jars) are standard in excavated Greek houses; and the related cult of Zeus Meilichios (the Kindly) is represented by a series of inscribed marble reliefs from the Piraeus, 4th c. BCE, showing an enormous bearded snake, labelled with the god's name. The household snake as an image of a property-and-prosperity Zeus is therefore attested in stone, not only in text — relevant to the agathodaemon serpent in Roman lararium painting, which is the same iconographic idea arrived at independently or by transmission.

Step 8. Zeus Herkeios (Zeus of the Enclosure) — the courtyard altar. Owning one was a test of citizenship:

"They ask him whether he has an Apollo Patroos and a Zeus Herkeios, and where these shrines are."

— Aristotle, Athenaion Politeia 55.3 (Tier A, my rendering), on the dokimasia (scrutiny) of incoming archons. This is the proof that a courtyard altar was normative in an Athenian house, not optional piety. In epic, Priam is killed at his: Euripides, Trojan Women 16–17 (Tier B).

Step 9. Hekate's suppers (deipna) at the new moon.

"If Hekate's supper were not set out for them, the poor would not snatch it up — and she would suffer no harm herself."

— Aristophanes, Plutus 594–597 (Tier B, compressed rendering). Food set at the crossroads or doorway at the noumenia (new moon, first of the month); the poor ate it, and everyone knew they did.

3.5 The monthly and annual cadence of the private household#

Step 10. The noumenia (νουμηνία, new-moon day, the 1st) is the household's monthly pivot. — Aristophanes, Clouds 1131–1200 (Tier B): the henē kai nea (ἕνη καὶ νέα, "old-and-new," the last day) and the noumenia structure the whole comic plot about debts, because they structured the real month.

Fixed monthly god-days attested at Athens: 1st noumenia; 2nd Agathos Daimon (Good Spirit); 3rd Athena; 4th Herakles, Hermes, Aphrodite, Eros; 6th Artemis; 7th Apollo; 8th Poseidon and Theseus.

⚠️ This list is a modern composite, assembled from comic fragments, scholia, and Philochorus. Individual entries are well attested; the list as a set is not a single ancient text. Hesiod, Works and Days 765–828 (the "Days") is Boeotian and agricultural, not Attic and cultic — do not conflate them.

On the posture of Greek prayer. The standing Homeric formula for praying is χεῖρας ἀνασχών, "having lifted up the hands" — the arms raised and the palms turned upward to the sky (Homer, Iliad 1.450, Chryses to Apollo; the same at 3.275, 6.301, 7.177). For the powers below the earth the posture inverts: the suppliant beats or presses the ground and the palms are turned down (Homer, Iliad 9.568–570, Althaea calling on Hades and Persephone; compare Iliad 14.272 and the tomb-rites of Aeschylus, Libation Bearers 84–164). Latin names the same gesture outright: supinas manus, the upturned palms (Vergil, Aeneid 3.176–177 and 4.205). This is one of the very few ritual details on which the Greek and Roman evidence agrees without qualification, which is why it is kept unchanged at §1.2.1.

The comic confirmation — the best procedural text#

Aristophanes, Peace 937–1126 (Tier B) runs the whole sequence on stage as a gag: fetching the sheep; the basket (kanoun) and barley; the water; the fire; the knife; cutting the hair and throwing it on the fire; sprinkling the altar; the prayer dictated line by line and repeated; the objection that the killing must happen offstage; the splanchna (entrails) brought out to be shared.

"Take the basket quickly, and the lustral water, and go round the altar to the right, fast." — Peace 956–957 (my rendering) "Dip in the brand and hold it out. Now you, throw the barley. And you, wash your hands and hand me the basin. Now scatter the barley among the audience." — Peace 959–962 (compressed)

Because the audience must follow the parody, the order is evidence.

Aristophanes, Birds 848–1057 (Tier B) preserves the form of a Greek priestly prayer at length — the piling up of deities and epithets, the "and to whomever else" clause — even though the content is absurd.

M for the whole sequence: the kanoun (three-handled flat basket), the machaira under the barley in it, the chernips basin, the fillets on the victim, the ololygē gesture (women's raised arms), the spitted splanchna over the altar fire, the osphys (tail) curling on the flame as the omen — all of these appear, repeatedly and consistently, on Attic red-figure sacrifice scenes from the 5th c. BCE. Vase-painting and Homer agree object for object. This is the strongest Tier M/Tier B convergence in Greek religion.

4. ROMAN HOUSEHOLD WORSHIP#

Far better documented than Greek, because of Cato — and far better preserved physically, because of Pompeii and Herculaneum.

4.1 The governing text#

Cato, De Agricultura 143 (Tier A), instructions to the vilica (the farm overseer's wife) — the only surviving ancient rule for domestic observance frequency:

Kalendis, Idibus, Nonis, festus dies cum erit, coronam in focum indat, per eosdemque dies lari familiari pro copia supplicet. "On the Kalends, the Ides, the Nones, and when there is a feast day, let her put a garland on the hearth, and on those same days let her make supplication to the Lar Familiaris according to her means."

And immediately after:

"Let her not perform any rite herself, nor have anyone else perform one for her, without the orders of the master or mistress. Let her know that the master performs the rites for the whole household."

Four load-bearing facts from this one chapter:

  1. Cadence: Kalends (1st), Nones (5th or 7th), Ides (13th or 15th), plus feriae (festival days). Not daily.
  2. Act: a garland (corona) on the hearth (focus), and supplication to the Lar Familiaris (the household's Lar).
  3. Authority: household religion is the office of the paterfamilias (male head of household). Others act only on his instruction.
  4. No words are given. Cato supplies verbatim prayers at 132, 134, 139, and 141 but not here. There is no attested daily prayer text to the Lares.

The material evidence for the lararium (Tier M) — the strongest evidence in this document#

Roughly 500 household shrines have been recorded at Pompeii and Herculaneum, buried in 79 CE and therefore a snapshot of ordinary practice rather than of ideal practice. What they show:

  • Three forms: the aedicula (miniature temple with columns and pediment on a podium), the wall-niche, and the plain painted panel. All three coexist, sometimes in the same house.
  • Placement: overwhelmingly in the kitchen and in service areas, and secondarily in the atrium and peristyle. This is a significant correction to the literary picture, which emphasizes the atrium: everyday household cult sat where the food was.
  • The standard painted program: a central Genius, togate, head veiled (capite velato), holding a patera (shallow offering dish) in one hand and often an acerra (incense box) in the other, flanked by two Lares in short tunics, dancing on tiptoe, each holding a rhyton (drinking horn) raised and a situla (bucket) lowered; below, one or two serpents (the agathodaemon, "good spirit") approaching a low altar bearing an egg and a pinecone. A tibicen (flute player) and a victimarius leading a pig appear in the fuller versions.
  • The House of the Vettii lararium (VI.15.1) is the type-specimen of the aedicula form with this program.
  • Physical residue: many niches were excavated containing lamps, coins, small bronze statuettes, incense burners, eggshell, pinecones, and burnt residue. Several contained statuettes of imported deities — Isis, Harpocrates, Mercury, Hercules, Fortuna — standing alongside the Lares.

Why this matters : the texts (Cato, Plautus) tell us when and what was offered; the paintings and deposits tell us what it looked like, where it stood, and that non-Roman gods were admitted to it. Neither source type says the other's half. No ancient text describes what a lararium painting should contain — that is known only from Tier M.

4.3 THE DAY — Morning#

Step 1. Washing on rising.

"...and you plunge your head in Tiber's stream two or three times in the morning, and wash away the night with the water."

— Persius, Satires 2.15–16 (Tier B, my rendering). Persius is attacking hypocritical prayer, which means the morning washing he describes is the ordinary expected practice.

Step 2. Prayer aloud, not muttered. Persius' whole second satire turns on the contrast between what a man will pray aloud and what he whispers so the gods alone hear — again, evidence for the norm: Roman prayer was spoken audibly and in fixed form.

Step 3. Greeting and offering at the shrine. The Lar of the house speaks the prologue of a comedy and states the frequency himself:

...quod mihi cottidie / aut ture aut vino aut aliqui semper supplicat, / dat mihi coronas. "...because she makes supplication to me every single day, with incense, or wine, or something, and gives me garlands."

— Plautus, Aulularia, prologue 23–25 (Tier B, c. 195 BCE). The god himself says cottidie — daily. This is the single most important Roman text after Cato 143, and it contradicts Cato's Kalends/Nones/Ides cadence. See §9.3 for the resolution.

The attested minimum offering is therefore this triad: incense (tus), wine (vinum), garland (corona). Confirmed materially by the incense burners, lamps, and floral garlands painted on the shrines themselves.

Step 4. The alternative, humble offering — spelt and salt.

mollivit aversos Penatis / farre pio et saliente mica "...has softened the Penates' turned-away faces with pious spelt and a leaping grain of salt."

— Horace, Odes 3.23.19–20 (Tier B). The whole ode is addressed to a country woman, Phidyle, and instructs her: if she lifts her upturned palms to the sky at the new moon and placates the Lares with incense, this year's grain, and a greedy pig, her vines will not suffer — and, more to the point, a costly victim is not required; clean hands and a grain of salt suffice. This is the clearest ancient statement that the scale of Roman household offering is discretionary.

4.4 THE DAY — Meals#

Step 5. A portion of the meal is placed in the hearth fire, in silence, and watched as an omen. — Servius, ad Aeneidem 1.730 (Tier C): at the second course silence was kept while a portion was put into the fire, and the household observed whether it flared. Pliny, NH 28.26–27 (Tier B) on ritual silence at table and the formula favete linguis (literally "be favourable with your tongues" — i.e. keep sacred silence).

Step 6. The household images brought to table.

"Three images of the Lares were brought in... and a bowl was passed around."

— Petronius, Satyricon 60.8 (Tier B, my rendering). At Trimalchio's dinner, three silver Lares images are carried in during the meal, along with a gold box holding the host's first beard-clippings, and the guests make obeisance. Petronius is satirizing a vulgar freedman, but the act is the ordinary one, over-performed.

Step 7. Offerings of first produce — the daps. Cato, De Agricultura 132 (Tier A) gives the offering to Jupiter Dapalis (Jupiter of the Feast) with the prayer verbatim:

Iuppiter dapalis, quod tibi fieri oportet in domo familia mea culignam vini dapi, eius rei ergo macte hac illace dape pollucenda esto. "Jupiter of the Feast, since it is fitting that a cup of wine be offered to you as a feast in my house and household — for this reason, be honoured by the offering of this feast."

And the instructions around it: wash your hands first; use wine of whatever quantity you wish; the day is a holiday for the oxen, the ox-drivers, and those making the offering; an offering to Vesta may be made at the same time if desired; the same wine may be used again afterward.

On reading the fire. The sign was heard as much as seen. Grains of mola salsa — toasted emmer ground with salt — crackle, spit and leap when they strike a hot flame, and Horace names the sound in the offering itself: farre pio et saliente mica, "with pious spelt and a leaping grain of salt" (Horace, Odes 3.23.20, Tier B). A flame that takes the offering brightly and crackles is the god feeding gladly; a flame that gutters and smothers it is the contrary sign. The same passage carries the proportionality doctrine: a guiltless hand with spelt and salt softens the Penates no less than a costly victim.

4.5 THE DAY — Evening#

⚠️ Honest gap. There is no Roman text prescribing an evening household rite comparable to Hesiod's "when you go to bed." The evening observance is the cena (dinner) offering of §4.4, since the cena was the evening meal. Any modern "Roman evening prayer" is filling a silence in the sources.

What is attested at day's end: lamp-lighting was itself marked. M: bronze and terracotta lamps are among the commonest finds in lararium niches, many with wicks burnt. The material record shows lamps burning at household shrines; it does not tell us what was said over them.

4.7 The Roman prayer form — the rules, which are themselves attested as rules#

Step 8. Cover the head — capite velato (with the head veiled).

purpureo velare comas adopertus amictu, / ne qua inter sanctos ignis in honore deorum / hostilis facies occurrat et omina turbet. "Veil your hair, covered with a purple robe, so that no hostile face may intrude among the holy fires in the worship of the gods and disturb the omens."

— Vergil, Aeneid 3.405–407 (Tier D, but presenting the normative Roman rule as ancestral instruction). Plutarch, Roman Questions 10 (Tier C) asks why Romans cover the head in worship and uncover before men, and offers several answers without choosing.

M: the veiled head is on the Ara Pacis processional frieze, on the Altar of Domitius Ahenobarbus, on Trajan's Column, on the Boscoreale cups, on countless coin reverses showing a togate figure at an altar, and on almost every Pompeian lararium Genius. This is one of the best-corroborated details in Roman religion.

Exception — the ritus Graecus (Greek rite). At the Ara Maxima of Hercules, and in the rites of Saturn, the head is uncovered. — Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.8.2 and 3.6.17 (Tier C); Servius, ad Aen. 3.407 (Tier C), who explicitly distinguishes ritus Graecus from ritus Romanus. Not a contradiction — a two-rite system Romans described themselves.

Step 8b. Raise the hands — manibus supinis (with upturned palms).

corripio e stratis corpus tendoque supinas / ad caelum cum voce manus… "I snatch my body from the bed and stretch my upturned hands to heaven with a cry…"

— Vergil, Aeneid 3.176–177 (Tier D); the same gesture at 4.205, where Iarbas prays with upturned palms at the altar. The Greek formula is identical in substance: χεῖρας ἀνασχών, "lifting up the hands" (Homer, Iliad 1.450). For the gods below the posture inverts — the palms turn downward and the hand strikes the earth (Homer, Iliad 9.568–570). M: the raised right hand of the worshipper is standard on Roman altar reliefs and on the adlocutio and sacrifice types of Imperial coinage.

Step 9. Speak the words exactly; a prompter guards them.

...de scripto praeire aliquem rursusque alium custodem dari qui adtendat, alium vero praeponi qui favere linguis iubeat, tibicinem canere ne quid aliud exaudiatur. "...that someone reads out the formula in advance from a script, that another is appointed as a guardian to attend to it, that yet another is set over them to command sacred silence, and that a flute-player plays so that nothing else can be heard."

— Pliny, Natural History 28.11 (Tier B). Pliny adds that if a word is omitted or spoken out of order the rite is void and must be begun again. This is the most important single passage on Roman ritual method in any source.

Step 10. Cover the uncertainty inside the formula.

Si deus, si dea es, quoium illud sacrum est, uti tibi ius est porco piaculo facere illiusce sacri coercendi ergo... "Whether you be god or goddess to whom this grove is sacred, as it is your right to have a pig offered to you as expiation for the clearing of this sacred place..."

— Cato, De Agricultura 139 (Tier A), before thinning a grove. The same open-ended construction appears in the archival evocatio formula at Macrobius, Sat. 3.9.7–8 (Tier C): "...and by whatever name it is lawful to call you." M: inscribed altars reading SEI DEO SEI DEIVAE SAC ("sacred, whether to a god or a goddess") survive from Rome — the practice is confirmed in stone.

Step 11. Face east; stand; turn a complete circle after praying (circumactio). — Pliny, NH 28.25 (Tier B): totum corpus circumagimus, "we turn the whole body around." Plutarch, Life of Numa 14 (Tier C) is the decisive text and shows the act is a full rotation rather than a turn toward the right hand: he asks why worshippers turn themselves round in a circle, and answers that it imitates the revolution of the universe — adding the alternative that the worshipper, having adored the god, turns with him and so completes the circle. Plutarch, Roman Questions 14 (Tier C) raises the same question. Vitruvius, De Architectura 4.5.1 (Tier B) prescribes that the temple and its image face west, so that a worshipper at the altar outside faces east toward the image — an architectural rule that encodes the ritual one. M: confirmed by temple orientations at Pompeii and Ostia, though with many exceptions dictated by street plan.

Step 12. The implements. Patera (shallow pouring dish), simpulum (small long-handled ladle), acerra (incense box), praefericulum (broad-mouthed jug), aspergillum (sprinkler), secespita (sacrificial knife), lituus (augur's curved staff). — Named in Varro, Festus, and Cicero, but the real evidence base is Tier M: these objects appear as a standing iconographic set on the reverses of Republican and Imperial coinage, on the pontifical implements carved on the Ara Pacis and on countless altars, and survive as excavated bronzes.

The single most useful chapter in either language#

Cato, De Agricultura 141 (Tier A) — the lustratio agri (purification of the fields) with the suovetaurilia (the triple offering of pig, sheep, and bull). It gives the circumambulation of the land, the prayer to Mars Pater verbatim, and — crucially — the repair clause for a failed rite:

Mars pater, si quid tibi in illisce suovitaurilibus lactentibus neque satisfactum est, te hisce suovitaurilibus piaculo. "Father Mars, if anything was not satisfactory to you in the offering of those sucklings, I make expiation to you with these offerings."

Read the chapter entire. It shows a Roman rite being constructed: what to say, in what order, what to do if the omens fail, and how to re-run it.

M: the suovetaurilia is the most frequently depicted Roman sacrifice in surviving art — the Altar of Domitius Ahenobarbus (Louvre, late 2nd–1st c. BCE), which shows the census lustrum with the three victims led by victimarii, the tibicen playing, and the veiled officiant pouring from a patera onto a portable altar; and repeatedly on Trajan's Column. Text and relief correspond act for act.

5. BIRTHS#

5.1 Greek (Athenian)#

Step 1. The birth goddesses. Eileithyia (or the Eileithyiai, plural in Homer); Artemis Lochia (of childbed) in tragedy.

"To you, Artemis, mistress of childbirth, the women who die in labour shall dedicate their garments."

— Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris 1462–1467 (Tier B, compressed rendering — Athena's prescription). Also Euripides, Hippolytus 166–169 (Tier B): Artemis is called the heavenly one who rules over labour pains. M: sanctuaries of Eileithyia and Artemis produce large deposits of votive textiles recorded on inventory inscriptions (Brauron, Attica: itemized lists of women's garments dedicated to Artemis, some naming the dedicator) — the material confirmation of exactly the practice Euripides describes.

Step 2. Announcement at the door. An olive wreath for a boy, a tuft of wool for a girl. — Hesychius s.v. στέφανον ἐκφέρειν (Tier C); scholion to Aristophanes, Lysistrata 757 (Tier C). The house is polluted (miaros, μιαρός — ritually unclean); the marker warns callers.

Step 3. Birth pollution (miasma, μίασμα). The procedural evidence is epigraphic:

"A woman in childbed pollutes the house. She pollutes the one who is inside the house and the one who goes in; she does not pollute anyone outside the house."

— The Cyrene cathartic law (SEG IX 72 / LSS 115, 4th c. BCE) (Tier A, my rendering). This is an actual inscribed sacred law from a Greek city, still legible, and it does something no literary source does: it defines the radius of pollution.

Step 4. The Amphidromia (Ἀμφιδρόμια, "running around") — 5th or 7th day. The infant is carried at a run around the burning hearth by the women who attended the birth, thereby purifying them and the house; friends send gifts; the door is wreathed.

The best evidence is an offhand metaphor, which proves the rite was universal:

"We must run around it in a circle, and see whether it is worth rearing, or a wind-egg and a falsehood."

— Plato, Theaetetus 160e–161a (Tier A, my rendering). Socrates says the newborn argument must be run around the hearth — a figure only intelligible if everyone present had performed the rite for actual children. The explicit description is at the scholion to this passage and at Hesychius s.v. ἀμφιδρόμια (Tier C); the customary gifts of octopus and cuttlefish come from the comic poets Ephippus and Eubulus quoted at Athenaeus 9.370c–d (Tier C).

⚠️ Conflict: fifth day or seventh? The lexicographers already disagreed. Probably real local or period variation. The hearth-circuit itself is uncontested.

Step 5. The dekatē (δεκάτη, "tenth") — tenth day: naming, sacrifice, feast. The legally decisive act.

"If she was his wedded wife and he had a child by her... he would have held the tenth-day feast, and invited his relatives."

— Isaeus 3.30 (On the Estate of Pyrrhus) (Tier A, compressed rendering). Demosthenes 39.20 and 40.28 (Tier A) turn on who was present at the naming and what name was given. The ritual was the registration system; there was no other.

Step 6. Introduction to the phratry (φρατρία, kin-brotherhood) at the Apatouria. The father swears over the meion (μεῖον, the infant sacrifice) that the child is his by a lawfully married Athenian woman; the phratry votes. — Demosthenes 43.11–14 and 57.54 (Tier A); Isaeus 8.19 (Tier A); Andocides 1.126 (Tier A). M: phratry decrees survive inscribed — the decrees of the Demotionidai (IG II² 1237, 396/5 BCE) set out the scrutiny procedure, the oath, and the penalties, in the phratry's own words.

5.2 Roman#

Step 1. Guarding the threshold on the night of the birth.

"Three men go round the house by night. First they strike the threshold with an axe, then with a pestle, then they sweep it with a broom — so that Silvanus may be kept from entering. From the cutting of the axe comes the god Intercidona; from the pestle, Pilumnus; from the broom, Deverra."

— Varro, Antiquitates Rerum Divinarum, preserved at Augustine, City of God 6.9 (Tier C preserving Tier A; my rendering). Augustine is hostile and mocking, but he is quoting Varro's antiquarian catalogue, and the detail is too specific and too useless to his argument to be invented.

Step 2. Juno Lucina, and the untying of knots.

"...and pray with your hair loosed, that she may gently release the burden."

— Ovid, Fasti 3.257–258 (Tier D, compressed rendering). The principle: bound things bind the birth. Pliny, NH 28.59 (Tier B) states that sitting with fingers interlaced near a pregnant woman is a hostile act, and that untying and re-tying knots affects delivery.

Step 3. Carmenta Prorsa and Postverta — two aspects of the goddess, for head-first and feet-first presentations. — Ovid, Fasti 1.617–636 (Tier D); Varro via Aulus Gellius, Noctes Atticae 16.16.4 (Tier C); Macrobius, Sat. 1.7.20 (Tier C).

Step 4. Lifting the child — tollere liberum ("to raise a child").

"...they decided to rear whatever she bore." (quidquid peperisset decreverunt tollere)

— Terence, Andria 219 (Tier B). ⚠️ The phrase is certain; a scripted ceremony is not. Tollere liberos is the ordinary Latin idiom for rearing children, used without any ritual context in Suetonius, Pliny's Letters, and the jurists. No ancient text describes a formal lifting rite with words. Do not build a liturgy on an idiom.

Step 5. The dies lustricus (day of purification) — the eighth day for girls, the ninth for boys.

"The day on which infants are purified is called lustricus. Girls are named on the eighth day, boys on the ninth."

— Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.16.36 (Tier C, my rendering). Plutarch, Roman Questions 102 (Tier C) asks why, and answers that the female develops faster — i.e. he is guessing. Festus s.v. lustrici dies (Tier C).

The rite involves sacrifice, the giving of the praenomen (personal name), and the placing of the bulla — a hollow locket-amulet worn until manhood. — Pliny, NH 33.10 (Tier B) on the bulla aurea (gold bulla) and its supposed origin with Tarquinius Priscus; Macrobius, Sat. 1.6.9–17 (Tier C) at length. M: gold and leather bullae survive in quantity; freeborn boys wear them in portrait sculpture and on sarcophagi, which is how the class distinction (gold for the wellborn, leather for others) is known.

Step 6. The childhood deities. The Roman habit of one god per function is genuine and catalogued — again hostilely — from Varro: Vaticanus (the first cry), Cunina (the cradle), Rumina (the breast), Educa and Potina (eating and drinking), Statilinus (standing), Fabulinus (first speech), Abeona and Adeona (going out and coming back). — Augustine, City of God 4.11, 4.21, 6.9, 7.3, quoting Varro (Tier C over Tier A).

6. WEDDINGS#

6.1 Athenian — the three days#

Day 1 — Proaulia (προαύλια, the day before)

Step 1. Engyē (ἐγγύη, the betrothal pledge) — the act that makes the marriage legal.

ταύτην γνησίων παίδων ἐπ' ἀρότῳ σοι δίδωμι. "I give you this woman for the ploughing of legitimate children."

— Menander, Dyskolos 842–844 (Tier B); the same formula at Perikeiromene 1013–1014 and Samia 726–727. The groom answers: "I take her."

"Whatever woman a father, or a brother by the same father, or a paternal grandfather gives out for lawful wedlock — from her the children shall be legitimate."

— the marriage law quoted at Demosthenes 46.18 (Tier A, my rendering).

Step 2. Proteleia (προτέλεια, preliminary offerings) to Artemis, Hera Teleia (of Fulfilment), Zeus Teleios, the Moirai, Aphrodite, and Peitho. — Euripides, Iphigenia at Aulis 718–719 (Tier B), where the word is used of the sacrifice made before a wedding; Pollux, Onomasticon 3.38 (Tier C).

Step 3. Dedication of childhood. Toys, girdle, and a lock of hair to Artemis.

"Timareta, before her wedding, dedicated to you, Artemis of the Lake, her tambourine, her ball, the net that held her hair, and her dolls, and their dresses."

— Anthologia Palatina 6.280 (Tier B, my rendering) — a dedicatory epigram, which is the direct evidence for the practice. Compare 6.276. Also Euripides, Hippolytus 1425–1427 (Tier B): the girls of Troezen cut their hair for Hippolytus before marriage. M: the Brauron sanctuary of Artemis produced mass deposits of girls' dedications, and the temple inventories inscribe the garments item by item.

Step 4. Loutrophoria (λουτροφορία, the carrying of the bath-water). Water for the nuptial bath fetched by a boy or girl relative in a tall two-handled loutrophoros (λουτροφόρος) from a named spring — at Athens, the Enneakrounos/Kallirrhoe.

"...and the Athenians still use it for the rites before marriage and for other sacred purposes."

— Thucydides 2.15.5 (Tier B, my rendering), of the Nine-Spouted Fountain. A contemporary, explicit, non-poetic statement. M: the loutrophoros is a unique vessel shape, and it does double duty — it was also set on the graves of those who died unmarried. Demosthenes 44.18 (Tier A) argues from a loutrophoros standing on a tomb that the deceased died unwed. Vessel shape, literary text, and legal argument all confirm one another.

Day 2 — Gamos (γάμος, the wedding itself)

Step 5. Sacrifice and feast at the bride's house. Doors garlanded with olive and laurel; sesame cakes served (sesame for fertility). — Aristophanes, Peace 869 and scholia (Tier B/C); Menander fr. 938 (Tier B).

A boy with both parents living (pais amphithalēs, παῖς ἀμφιθαλής), crowned with thorns and acorns, carries bread from a winnowing basket and says:

ἔφυγον κακόν, εὗρον ἄμεινον. "I fled the worse; I found the better."

— Zenobius 3.98 (Tier C). One of the very few verbatim Greek wedding utterances preserved.

Step 6. Anakalyptēria (ἀνακαλυπτήρια, the unveiling). The bride raises her veil before the groom and receives gifts. — Pollux 2.59, 3.36 (Tier C). M: this is the central image of Attic wedding iconography — the bride's hand at her veil, on pyxides, lebetes gamikoi (wedding bowls), and loutrophoroi, from the mid-5th c. BCE onward. The gesture is known far better from vases than from texts.

Step 7. The quince.

"Solon directed that the bride should nibble a quince before entering the bridal chamber."

— Plutarch, Solon 20.3 (Tier C, my rendering); repeated at Roman Questions 65 and Advice to Bride and Groom (Moralia 138d) (Tier C). Attributed to Solon's legislation, so plausibly Tier A in origin.

Step 8. The torchlit procession (pompē) to the groom's house.

"...and they were leading the brides from their chambers through the city under blazing torches, and the wedding song rose loud... and the women stood, each at her door, and marvelled."

— Homer, Iliad 18.491–496 (Tier B, my rendering — the wedding scene on the Shield of Achilles). The cry is Hymēn, Hymenai' ō (Ὑμήν, Ὑμέναι' ὦ). The actual hymns survive: Sappho fr. 44 (the wedding of Hector and Andromache), fr. 105a, 111, 112, 114 (Tier B). Parodies with full structure: Aristophanes, Peace 1332–1359 and Birds 1720–1765 (Tier B). M: the mule-cart procession with the bride between groom and parochos (best man), the torch-bearing mother, and the nympheutria (bridal attendant) is a fixed scene type on Attic black- and red-figure vases.

Step 9. Katachysmata (καταχύσματα, the showering). On arrival the couple goes to the hearth and is showered with dried fruit, nuts, dates, figs, and small coins. — Scholion to Aristophanes, Plutus 768 (Tier C). The same rite welcomed a newly purchased slave into the house, which is why Aristophanes can joke about it — an unintended confirmation that the hearth is the point of entry into a household for any new member.

Day 3 — Epaulia (ἐπαύλια, the day after)

Step 10. Gifts brought to the bride in procession. — Pollux 3.39; Harpocration s.v. ἐπαύλια (Tier C).

Step 11. The gamēlia (γαμηλία) — the husband presents the marriage to his phratry at the next Apatouria. The legal proof of marriage.

"He never gave the marriage-feast to the phratry members on her behalf."

— Isaeus 3.76 (Tier A, my rendering), used to prove no marriage existed. Also Isaeus 8.18.

6.2 Roman#

Step 1. Choosing the day — the prohibitions.

mense malum Maio nubere vulgus ait. "The common people say it is bad to marry in the month of May."

— Ovid, Fasti 5.490 (Tier D).

"...until the calm Tiber has carried the sweepings from Ilian Vesta down to the sea on his yellow waters."

— Ovid, Fasti 6.227–228 (Tier D, my rendering), the flaminica Dialis explaining why she neither combs her hair nor cuts her nails, and why there is to be no marrying, until June 15.

Forbidden: all of May; June 1–14; the Kalends, Nones, and Ides and the day after each (dies postriduani, "the days after," reckoned atri, black); Feb 13–21 (dies parentales, the ancestral days); the days the mundus stood open (Aug 24, Oct 5, Nov 8); the Salian processions in March and October. — Macrobius, Sat. 1.15.21–22 and 1.16.21–24 (Tier C); Plutarch, Roman Questions 86 and 105 (Tier C). Widows, notably, could marry on the forbidden days — Plutarch, RQ 105. M: the Fasti Antiates Maiores — a painted pre-Julian calendar from Antium, the only surviving pre-Caesarian Roman calendar — and the Fasti Praenestini, Verrius Flaccus' own calendar with his commentary carved beside the days, mark the character of each day (F, N, NP, EN, C) directly. These are the primary evidence for which days were legally and religiously available, and they outrank every literary statement about it.

Step 2. Auspices taken. An auspex nuptiarum (wedding augur) attends and declares the omens good; by Cicero's day the office was formal rather than divinatory. — Cicero, De Divinatione 1.28 (Tier B); Juvenal 10.336 (Tier B); Valerius Maximus 2.1.1 (Tier C).

Step 3. The bride's dress, prepared the evening before.

  • Tunica recta ("upright tunic") — woven in one piece on the old vertical loom. — Pliny, NH 8.194 (Tier B): brides wear it, and Tanaquil was said to have woven the first. Her childhood toys and bulla are dedicated to the Lares the night before.
  • Cingulum (belt) of wool tied in the nodus Herculaneus (Hercules knot), which only the husband may untie. — Festus s.v. cingillo (Tier C); Pliny, NH 28.63 (Tier B) on the healing virtue of the Herculean knot.
  • Seni crines ("six locks") — the hair divided with the hasta caelibaris (the bachelor spear, preferably one drawn from a dead gladiator).

comat virgineas hasta recurva comas. "A bent spear-point dresses the maiden's hair."

— Ovid, Fasti 2.560 (Tier D). Festus s.v. caelibari hasta (Tier C). Plutarch, Roman Questions 87 (Tier C) offers four explanations and settles on none — the act survived, the reason did not.

  • Flammeum (flame-coloured veil) — the same veil worn by the flaminica Dialis. — Festus s.v. flammeo; Pliny, NH 21.46 (Tier B) on the colour.
  • Corona (wreath) of verbena and marjoram, gathered by the bride herself. — Catullus 61.6–7 (Tier B); Festus s.v. corollam.

Step 4. The ceremony at the bride's house.

  • The pronuba — a woman married only once (univira) — joins the couple's right hands (dextrarum iunctio). — Servius, ad Aen. 4.166 (Tier C). M: the joined right hands are the single most common image on Roman marriage sarcophagi and on the dextrarum iunctio coin type; the pronuba stands behind with a hand on each shoulder.
  • Tabulae nuptiales (marriage contract) sealed before witnesses.
  • Confarreatio — the patrician form, ten witnesses, a cake of far (emmer wheat):

"...by confarreatio, which is done through a kind of sacrifice made to Jupiter Farreus, in which a cake of spelt is employed... and many other things are done and said, with set and solemn words, in the presence of ten witnesses."

— Gaius, Institutes 1.112 (Tier A, my rendering). Pliny, NH 18.10 (Tier B) adds that nothing was held more sacred than this bond, and that the couple shared the spelt cake.

  • The bride's consent formula:

Ubi tu Gaius, ego Gaia. "Where you are Gaius, I am Gaia."

— Plutarch, Roman Questions 30 (Tier C); Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria 1.7.28 (Tier B), who discusses the archaic spelling of Gaia in the formula — proof it was still in live use. ⚠️ The sources place the utterance at different moments (contract vs. entering the house). Plutarch has it at the entry.

Step 5. The deductio in domum (leading to the house) — after dark.

  • The mock seizure of the bride from her mother's arms. — Festus s.v. rapi (Tier C); Catullus 61.3–4, 56–59 (Tier B).
  • The shout Talassio! — Livy 1.9.12 (Tier B), with a Sabine-women aetiology; Plutarch, Romulus 15 (Tier C), who gives five competing explanations. The cry is certain; its meaning was already lost.
  • Fescennina iocatio — obscene joking verses along the route. The actual verses survive: Catullus 61.119–143 (Tier B).
  • Nuts thrown to the children:

sparge, marite, nuces. "Scatter nuts, bridegroom."

— Vergil, Eclogue 8.30 (Tier D); Catullus 61.124–128 (Tier B).

  • Five torches of spina alba (whitethorn). — Pliny, NH 16.75 (Tier B): whitethorn torches are carried at weddings as the most auspicious of all. Plutarch, RQ 2 (Tier C) asks why five and not more or fewer, and does not know.
  • Three boys patrimi et matrimi (with both parents living): two lead her by the hand, one carries the torch. — Festus s.v. patrimi (Tier C); Varro via Servius, ad Aen. 4.167 (Tier C).
  • A camillus (boy attendant) carries the cumerus (covered basket of the bride's implements). — Festus s.v. cumeram (Tier C).

Step 6. At the groom's door.

  • The bride winds wool round the doorposts and anoints them with fat. Romans derived uxor (wife) from unguere (to anoint) on the strength of it. — Pliny, NH 28.135 and 28.142 (Tier B): wolf's fat is used by brides on the doorposts; Servius, ad Aen. 4.458 (Tier C) for the etymology.
  • She is carried over the threshold, never touching it:

transfer omine cum bono / limen aureolos pedes, / rasilemque subi forem. "With good omen carry your golden feet over the threshold, and pass beneath the polished door."

— Catullus 61.159–161 (Tier B). Plutarch, Roman Questions 29 (Tier C) gives three explanations and prefers none.

  • She is given fire and water — the two elements that constitute membership in a household.

Διὰ τί τὴν γαμουμένην ἅπτεσθαι πυρὸς καὶ ὕδατος κελεύουσιν; "Why do they bid the bride touch fire and water?"

— Plutarch, Roman Questions 1 (Tier C) — the very first question in the collection. Varro, De Lingua Latina 5.61 (Tier B) gives the elemental rationale. The proof it was real: banishment was legally expressed as aquae et ignis interdictio (the forbidding of water and fire) — the exact inverse rite.

Step 7. The lectus genialis — the marriage bed, sacred to the husband's Genius, standing in the atrium facing the door. — Festus s.v. genialis lectus (Tier C); Horace, Epistles 1.1.87 (Tier B).

Step 8. The repotia — next day, a second dinner, and the bride's first offering as materfamilias (mistress of the household) at the hearth. — Festus s.v. repotia (Tier C); Horace, Satires 2.2.60 (Tier B).

7. FUNERALS#

7.1 Greek (Athenian)#

Step 1. Closing the eyes and mouth at the moment of death.

"He uncovered his face — for he had covered it — and said this, which was his last utterance... and Crito closed his mouth and his eyes."

— Plato, Phaedo 118a (Tier A, my rendering). Also Homer, Odyssey 11.425–426 (Tier B).

Step 2. The fare for the ferryman — two obols.

"...a ferryman will take you across in a little boat, for two obols."

— Aristophanes, Frogs 139–140 (Tier B, my rendering). The satirist confirms the sum was proverbial. M: coins in the mouth or hand of the deceased are recovered from Greek graves across the Mediterranean, in enough cases to establish the practice, though far from universally — which is itself informative: the rite was normal but not obligatory.

Step 3. Prothesis (πρόθεσις, the laying-out) — washing, anointing, dressing, by the women of the household. Body washed, anointed with oil, dressed (at Athens, by Solon's law, in not more than three garments), crowned, and laid on a bier with the feet toward the door. A vessel of water fetched from outside the house — the ardanion (ἀρδάνιον) — stands at the door so visitors can purify themselves on leaving. Branches of celery or cypress mark the house. — Lucian, On Funerals (De Luctu) 11–20 (Tier B) for the whole sequence; Pollux 8.65 (Tier C) for the ardanion.

"A honey-cake for me, and set a wreath on my head, and a jar of water at the door."

— Aristophanes, Ecclesiazusae 1030–1033 (Tier B, compressed rendering) — a joke that only works because the three objects were standard.

M — the strongest funerary evidence in Greek art: the prothesis is the primary subject of the monumental Dipylon kraters and amphorae from the Kerameikos cemetery at Athens (8th c. BCE) — the corpse on a high bier under a chequered shroud, mourners on both sides with both hands raised to the head, the bier legs, the attendants beneath. The scene recurs on black-figure funerary plaques (pinakes) of the 6th c. and on white-ground lekythoi of the 5th. Feet-toward-the-door, the wreath, and the mourners' tearing gesture are all visible. Four centuries of iconographic continuity, independent of any text.

Step 4. Lamentation — thrēnos (θρῆνος, the sung dirge) and goos (γόος, the kinsmen's wail). — Homer, Iliad 24.719–776 (Tier B) — the laments of Andromache, Hecuba, and Helen over Hector; the model text, with the professional singers leading and the women answering. Euripides, Alcestis 425–434 (Tier B): Admetus orders a year of mourning, the cutting of hair, and the shearing of the horses' manes.

Step 5. Ekphora (ἐκφορά, the carrying-out) — before sunrise, on the day after the laying-out. The law is read aloud in court, verbatim:

"The deceased shall be laid out inside the house in whatever manner one wishes. They shall carry out the dead on the day after the laying-out, before the sun rises. The men shall walk in front, the women behind. No woman under sixty years of age shall be permitted to enter the chamber of the deceased or to follow the body when it is carried to the tomb, except those within the degree of children of cousins."

— Solon's funerary law, quoted at Demosthenes 43.62 (Against Macartatus) (Tier A, my rendering). The clerk reads the actual statute.

Plutarch, Solon 21 (Tier C) adds the bans on laceration of the flesh, on set lamentations, and on sacrificing an ox at the tomb. Cicero, De Legibus 2.59–66 (Tier B) discusses the Athenian sumptuary funeral laws and compares them to the Twelve Tables.

Step 6. Burial or cremation, with offerings. The full literary model is Homer, Iliad 23.108–257 (Patroclus): the hundred-foot pyre, the jars of honey and oil set against the bier, the animals killed, the fire quenched with wine, the bones gathered in a double layer of fat into a golden urn, the mound heaped over. Also Iliad 24.782–804 (Hector, in brief).

Step 7. Purification of house and mourners. Again the procedural text is inscribed, not literary:

"The corpse shall be carried out covered, in silence, all the way to the grave... After the funeral, the house shall be purified with sea-water, and then smeared over with earth... The women who come to the funeral shall depart before the men. There shall be no rites on the thirtieth day."

— The sacred law of Iulis on Keos (IG XII 5, 593, 5th c. BCE) (Tier A, my rendering). Note it also caps the shrouds at three, worth no more than 300 drachmae.

"The women who mourn shall complete the purification of the shrines on the tenth day..."

— The law of Gambreion (LSAM 16, 3rd c. BCE) (Tier A, compressed) — which sets mourning at three months for women, four for men, and names the official responsible for enforcing it.

Step 8. Perideipnon (περίδειπνον, the funeral meal), held in the house, with the dead man as notional host. — Lucian, De Luctu 24 (Tier B); Demosthenes 18.288 (Tier A).

Step 9. The subsequent days: trita (third), enata (ninth), triakostia (thirtieth).

"He performed the ninth-day rites and all the other customary observances at the tomb."

— Isaeus 8.39 (Tier A, my rendering) — used as evidence of legitimate heirship. Also Isaeus 2.36–37. ⚠️ Direct conflict: Athens performs the thirtieth-day rite; the Keian law above forbids it. This is a genuine polis-level legal difference, not textual corruption.

Step 10. Annual commemoration — the Genesia (Boedromion 5 at Athens). — Herodotus 4.26 (Tier B) treats it as a general Greek institution; Philochorus via Hesychius s.v. γενέσια (Tier C) gives the Attic date.

7.2 Roman#

Step 1. The last breath, and the conclamatio (the calling-out). The nearest kinsman catches the last breath; the body is then called by name aloud at intervals. — Vergil, Aeneid 4.684–685 (Tier D) for the last breath. Servius, ad Aen. 6.218 (Tier C) explains that the dead was called by name repeatedly so that no one should be burnt alive, and that from this comes the phrase conclamatum est ("it has been called out") for a lost cause.

Step 2. The body laid on the ground, washed with warm water, anointed. — Servius, ad Aen. 6.218 (Tier C); Pliny, NH 7.46 (Tier B).

Step 3. Collocatio — laying out in the atrium, feet toward the door, on the lectus funebris (funeral couch), for up to seven days. Cypress or pitch-pine at the door marks a house of death.

"...and it is placed at the door of a house where a death has occurred."

— Pliny, NH 16.139 (Tier B, my rendering), of the cypress, which he says is sacred to Dis. The undertaker, the libitinarius, operated from the grove of Venus Libitina; Dionysius of Halicarnassus 4.15.5 (Tier B) reports that Servius Tullius required a coin paid to Libitina for every death, as a running census — which incidentally means Rome had death registration.

M: the Tomb of the Haterii relief (Rome, late 1st c. CE, Vatican Museums) shows the collocatio directly: the deceased on a high draped couch, torches burning at the corners, garlands, professional mourners with loosened hair beating their breasts, flute-players, and household members. It is the closest thing to a photograph of a Roman lying-in-state.

Step 4. Os resectum — a bone cut off before cremation and separately buried.

"...nor is the family held to be in mourning until, a bone having been cut off, the funeral rites have been performed with it."

— Cicero, De Legibus 2.22.55 (Tier B, my rendering), quoting the pontifical rule. The point is technical: cremation destroys the body, but a grave becomes religiosus (consecrated) only by burial of a body part. Festus s.v. ossum resectum and s.v. membrum abscidi (Tier C); Varro, De Lingua Latina 5.23 (Tier B).

Step 5. Pompa funebris (the funeral procession). The fullest description in either literature, by an eyewitness at a 2nd-c. BCE aristocratic funeral:

"Whenever any distinguished man dies, he is carried in the procession into the forum to the Rostra, sometimes upright and conspicuous, rarely reclining. With the whole people standing round, his son... mounts the Rostra and speaks about the virtues of the dead man and what he accomplished in life... Then, after burying him and performing the customary rites, they place the image of the dead man in the most conspicuous part of the house, enclosed in a little wooden shrine. The image is a mask, made to resemble him with remarkable fidelity in shape and colour. On the occasion of public sacrifices they display these images and decorate them with care. And when any distinguished member of the family dies, they carry them in the funeral procession, putting them on men who seem most like the originals in height and bearing. These wear togas with a purple border if the man was consul or praetor, whole purple if censor, gold-embroidered if he had celebrated a triumph. They ride in chariots, with the fasces and axes and the other insignia customarily carried before the magistrates... There could hardly be a more inspiring sight for a young man who aspires to fame and virtue."

— Polybius 6.53.1–6.54.3 (Tier B, my rendering, condensed). This one passage supplies: the upright body, the Rostra, the son's eulogy, the imagines (ancestor masks), their storage in household shrines, their use at sacrifices, the casting of wearers by build, the office-correct dress, the chariots, and the explicit civic purpose.

Also in the procession: torches (carried even by day — evidence the rite was once nocturnal, per Servius, ad Aen. 11.143, Tier C); tibicines (flute-players), capped at ten by the Twelve Tables; praeficae (hired mourning women) singing the nenia (dirge); and an archimimus (chief mime) impersonating the dead man's speech and gait — Suetonius, Vespasian 19.2 (Tier B), where the mime at Vespasian's funeral jokes about the cost of the funeral, in character.

M: the Amiternum relief (1st c. BCE, Aquila) shows the procession from the side — the bier under a canopy on a wheeled platform, tibicines and cornicines (horn players) in front, praeficae with raised arms behind, and the family following. Text and relief correspond figure for figure.

Step 6. Laudatio funebris (funeral eulogy) from the Rostra. — Polybius 6.53.2–3 (Tier B); Cicero, Brutus 61–62 (Tier B), who complains these speeches had corrupted Roman history with invented consulships and false triumphs. M: the Laudatio Turiae (CIL VI 1527, late 1st c. BCE) is a funeral eulogy for a wife, inscribed at length on stone by her husband — an actual surviving laudatio, and by far the longest private inscription from Rome.

Step 7. Cremation or burial — outside the walls.

Hominem mortuum in urbe ne sepelito neve urito. "A dead man shall not be buried nor burnt within the city."

— Twelve Tables X.1, quoted at Cicero, De Legibus 2.23.58 (Tier A within Tier B).

The pyre sequence: the eyes are opened on the pyre; the nearest kin lights it with a torch, face turned away; spices, garments, and gifts are thrown on; the fire is quenched with wine; the bones are washed in wine and milk, dried in linen, and placed in the urn. — Vergil, Aeneid 6.212–235 (Tier D) — the funeral of Misenus, which is a complete Roman funeral in Homeric dress: the pyre of oak and pine, the cypress, the body on the couch above, the cauldrons of warm water for washing and anointing, the wailing, the averted face at the lighting, the frankincense and bowls of oil, the bones gathered and quenched in wine, the urn, and the mound with the man's oar and trumpet set on it. Pliny, NH 14.88 (Tier B) on the wine-quenching.

M: the ustrina (cremation platforms) of the Imperial family on the Campus Martius are excavated; the Isola Sacra necropolis at Portus and the Vatican necropolis preserve tombs with cenae (dining) facilities, pipes (profusiones) leading from the surface into the burial for pouring offerings to the dead, and inscriptions specifying who may dine there and on what days. The tombs physically encode the calendar of the dead.

Step 8. Porca praesentanea — a sow sacrificed to Ceres in the presence of the body, before burial. — Cicero, De Legibus 2.22.55 (Tier B); Festus s.v. praesentanea porca (Tier C).

Step 9. Purification: the suffitio, the everriae, the silicernium. Mourners are sprinkled with water and step over fire (suffitio). The house is swept out with a special broom by a designated person to expel the dead:

"The everriator is the one who obtains the inheritance by right, and who must sweep out the house with a certain kind of broom as part of the funeral rite."

— Festus s.v. everriator (Tier C, my rendering). Note the legal linkage: sweeping and inheriting are the same office. The silicernium is the funeral feast eaten at the grave. — Festus s.v. silicernium (Tier C).

Step 10. Novemdiale sacrificium — the ninth day. A sacrifice at the tomb ending the mourning, followed by the cena novendialis (ninth-day dinner). — Porphyrio on Horace, Epodes 17.48 (Tier C); Tacitus, Annals 6.5 (Tier B); Apuleius, Metamorphoses 9.31 (Tier B). The state form of the same rite, after prodigies, at Livy 1.31.4 (Tier B).

Step 11. Annual commemoration: the Parentalia and the Lemuria. See §8.1.

7.3 The one text to read whole#

Cicero, De Legibus 2.19–27 (Tier B) is a continuous legal-religious treatment: the ius Manium (the law of the ancestral dead), the definition of when ground becomes religiosus, the Twelve Tables' sumptuary limits, the rule that a grave is consecrated only by the burial of a body or bone, the transmission of the sacra with the inheritance, and the limits on tomb construction. No other single Latin text does this much work.

8. FESTIVALS WITH RECOVERABLE RITUAL DETAIL#

Selected on one criterion: an ancient text gives enough procedure to perform the rite. Festivals known only by name are excluded. Roman festivals first, in calendar order; then Greek, in Attic calendar order; then non-Attic Greek.

8.1 ROMAN — in calendar order#

COMPITALIA — movable; announced by the praetor, falling between late December and early January#

The rule:

Rem divinam nisi Compitalibus in compito aut in foco ne faciat. "Let him perform no rite except at the Compitalia, at the crossroads or at the hearth."

— Cato, De Agricultura 5.3 (Tier A), of the farm overseer. Crossroads and hearth are the two lawful sites of worship for anyone who is not the paterfamilias.

The procedure:

  • Shrines (compita) stand where properties meet, with an opening on each of the four sides so that each household's Lar can pass. — Dionysius of Halicarnassus 4.14.3–4 (Tier B), attributing the institution to Servius Tullius, and noting that slaves take part, having laid aside their servile dress.
  • A woollen figure (effigies) is hung up for every free member of the household, and a woollen ball (pila) for every slave.

"Balls and figures are hung at the Compitalia in the streets, because this day is a festival of the Lares, whose divinity is thought to be concerned with the dead — so many balls as there are slaves, and so many figures as there are free persons."

— Festus s.v. pilae et effigies (Tier C, my rendering). The shrine is thus a census of the household presented to the Lares.

  • Games (ludi compitalicii) follow; the day is a general holiday.

M: Delos preserves a series of compital shrines in the residential quarters, painted with sacrifice scenes showing the ministri (attendants) at the altar with pig, ram, and bull. At Pompeii, painted street-corner shrines survive on the exterior walls of houses, showing two Lares flanking a Genius above a large serpent. At Rome, the Augustan vicomagistri altars (e.g. the Belvedere Altar, Vatican) show the reorganized cult of the Lares Augusti, with the Genius Augusti and the crossroads officials. The crossroads cult is better attested archaeologically than textually.

PARENTALIA — February 13–21, closing with FERALIA on the 21st and CARISTIA on the 22nd#

What is suspended: magistrates lay aside their insignia, temples close, altars have no fire, and no marriages are performed.

The offering — deliberately modest:

parva petunt manes: pietas pro divite grata est / munere; non avidos Styx habet ima deos. / tegula porrectis satis est velata coronis / et sparsae fruges parcaque mica salis / inque mero mollita Ceres violaeque solutae "The Shades ask little: dutifulness is more welcome to them than a costly gift; the Styx below does not hold greedy gods. A tile veiled with laid-out wreaths is enough, and scattered grain, and a stingy grain of salt, and bread softened in wine, and loose violets."

— Ovid, Fasti 2.535–539 (Tier D). This is the most quotable statement of the Roman principle that correct form outweighs expense — the same principle as Horace's grain of salt at Odes 3.23.

Ovid adds (2.547–556) the aetiology that when the rites were once neglected during a war, the city was troubled at night by the wailing of ghosts in the streets.

The Feralia rite to Tacita/Muta (Feb 21) — a private spell performed by an old woman, and one of the very few magical procedures preserved in a mainstream Latin text:

  • three grains of incense placed under the threshold, where a mouse has made a secret hole;
  • dark threads bound onto a spinning lead figure;
  • seven black beans turned over in her mouth;
  • the head of a small fish, its mouth sewn shut with bronze thread and smeared with pitch, roasted;
  • wine poured and drunk, and the words: "We have bound hostile tongues and unfriendly mouths."

— Ovid, Fasti 2.571–582 (Tier D, condensed rendering).

The Caristia / Cara Cognatio (Feb 22) — the day after the dead are dismissed, the living family dines together:

"...let the household gods receive incense, and let food be set before the kindly Lares."

— Ovid, Fasti 2.631–634 (Tier D, compressed). Quarrelling kin are told explicitly to stay away.

M: tomb inscriptions across the Empire endow Parentalia observances by name and specify what is to be spent, e.g. legacies funding roses and a meal at the tomb on fixed days. The profusio pipes at Isola Sacra and the Vatican necropolis are the plumbing for exactly the wine-and-food offerings Ovid describes. The Fasti Antiates Maiores and other painted and inscribed calendars mark the whole span Feb 13–21 as dies parentales.

LUPERCALIA — February 15 (falls inside the Parentalia period)#

  1. Sacrifice of goats and a dog at the Lupercal.
  2. The bloody knife is touched to the foreheads of two youths of noble family; the blood is wiped off with wool dipped in milk; the youths are required to laugh.
  3. A feast.
  4. The Luperci cut the goatskins into thongs (februa, instruments of purification) and run nearly naked around the Palatine, striking those they meet.

"...and many of the women of rank deliberately put themselves in the way and hold out their hands to be struck, like children in school, believing that this helps the pregnant to an easy delivery, and the barren to conceive."

— Plutarch, Caesar 61 (Tier C, my rendering). Plutarch had seen the festival. Also Plutarch, Romulus 21 (Tier C) for the blood-and-wool-and-laughter sequence; Ovid, Fasti 2.267–452 (Tier D) at length. Plutarch, Roman Questions 68 (Tier C) asks why a dog is killed and offers several answers, none confident.

TERMINALIA — February 23#

An operational neighbour-rite, given step by step:

  1. Neighbours meet at the shared boundary stone (terminus) and garland it on both sides.
  2. An altar is built on the spot. The farmer's wife brings fire from the household hearth on a broken potsherd; an old man splits wood and drives in stakes.
  3. He kindles the fire with dry bark; a boy holds out a wide basket and throws grain into the flames three times; a girl offers honeycomb.
  4. Others hold out wine; a portion is poured into the fire.
  5. The assembled neighbours, dressed in white, keep silence during the offering; a lamb or suckling pig is offered; then they feast and sing to Terminus.

— Ovid, Fasti 2.639–678 (Tier D, condensed rendering).

  1. The law:

"...he who has ploughed up the boundary stone is accursed, and so are his oxen."

— Ovid, Fasti 2.657–658 (Tier D, compressed), reporting an archaic sacral law also attested for the regal period.

M: inscribed termini (boundary stones) survive in quantity, including the cippi of the Tiber banks and the pomerium markers — the object at the centre of this rite is an ordinary, abundant, excavated artefact.

FORDICIDIA — April 15#

Pregnant cows (fordae) are sacrificed — one in each of the thirty curiae (wards) and one on the Capitol by the pontiffs. The unborn calves are taken from the wombs and burnt by the eldest Vestal, and the ashes are kept for the Parilia six days later. — Ovid, Fasti 4.629–672 (Tier D). This material linkage between two festivals is rare and specific, and it is what makes the Parilia recipe below possible.

PARILIA / PALILIA — April 21#

The most completely preserved Roman agricultural rite. Ovid, Fasti 4.721–782 (Tier D); corroborated at Tibullus 2.5.87–90 and Propertius 4.4.73–78 (Tier D).

  1. Obtain the suffimen (fumigant) from the Vestals: the ashes of the unborn calves from the Fordicidia, the blood of the October Horse, and bean-straw.
  2. Purify the fold at dawn: sweep the ground, sprinkle it with water, and deck the fold with leaves and branches; garland the door with a long wreath.
  3. Make blue smoke with pure sulphur, and make the sheep bleat in the smoke.
  4. Burn male olive, pine, savin, and laurel until it crackles.
  5. Offer baskets of millet, millet cakes, and pails of milk, warmed.
  6. Pray four times, facing east, at the touch of dew, washing the hands in living water. The prayer (given entire at 4.747–776) asks Pales to pardon unwitting trespass on sacred ground, to spare flock and shepherd, and to keep sickness, wolves, and hunger from the folds:

"Drive off both wolves and sickness; let the healthy sheep wander safe. Let there be much fodder, much water; let the udders be heavy with milk." (compressed rendering)

  1. Drink the burranica — milk mixed with sapa (boiled must).
  2. Leap three times over crackling heaps of burning straw.

ROBIGALIA — April 25#

Ovid reports an encounter with the actual procession and an exchange with the actual priest — the most valuable single passage in the Fasti:

"I was returning to Rome from Nomentum... when a crowd in white robes blocked the middle of the road. A flamen was going to the grove of ancient Mildew, to give the entrails of a dog and the entrails of a sheep to the flames. I went up at once, so as not to be ignorant of the rite."

— Ovid, Fasti 4.905–910 (Tier D, my rendering). The location is the fifth milestone on the Via Claudia.

The prayer of the flamen Quirinalis:

"Scaly Mildew, spare the sprouting corn, and let the smooth tip quiver on the surface of the ground... You have power enough: attack iron rather than the crops. Destroy first whatever can destroy. It is better that you gnaw swords and harmful weapons — there is no need of them; the world is at peace."

— Ovid, Fasti 4.911–932 (Tier D, condensed rendering).

Ovid then asks the priest why a dog is offered and is given an answer about the Dog Star. This is a recorded interview with a serving Roman priest, which no other source in this document can claim.

LEMURIA — May 9, 11, 13#

The most completely preserved private ritual in Latin literature. Ovid, Fasti 5.429–444 (Tier D, and no competing account exists):

  1. Rise at midnight, in silence.
  2. Go barefoot; no knots, no bindings.
  3. Make the sign of the fica — the thumb thrust between closed fingers — to avert any shade encountered.
  4. Wash the hands clean in spring water.
  5. Turn; take black beans into the mouth; throw them behind you without looking back, saying:

haec ego mitto; his, inquit, redimo meque meosque fabis. "These I cast; with these beans I redeem myself and mine."

  1. Say it nine times. Do not look back. The shade is believed to gather the beans and follow unseen.
  2. Touch water again.
  3. Clash bronze — "the bronze of Temesa."
  4. Ask the shade to leave:

manes exite paterni. "Ancestral spirits, go out."

  1. Say it nine times; then look back. The rite is complete and purely performed.

Ovid, Fasti 5.485–490 (Tier D) connects these days to the closing of the temples and the prohibition on marrying in May. The bean is a food of the dead — Pliny, NH 18.118 (Tier B) records its use in rites for the departed and the Pythagorean avoidance of it.

AMBARVALIA — movable, late May#

No prose instruction survives, but the operational description does:

"Let the whole crowd of your farmhands worship Ceres; for her, mix honeycomb with milk and soft wine, and let the auspicious victim be led three times around the young crops, with all the company following in chorus and shouting, and calling Ceres into their houses."

— Vergil, Georgics 1.343–347 (Tier D, my rendering).

The private version of the same act is Cato, De Agricultura 141 (Tier A), the lustratio agri with the suovetaurilia — the same circumambulation, on one farm, with the words supplied. Use Cato for the words and Vergil for the scale.

VESTALIA — June 7–15#

  • The inner shrine (penus Vestae) is opened only to matrons, who enter barefoot.
  • There is no image. The fire is the goddess:

nec tu aliud Vestam quam vivam intellege flammam. "Understand Vesta as nothing other than living flame."

— Ovid, Fasti 6.291 (Tier D), and at 6.295–298 that no body should be imagined for her.

  • Millers and bakers keep holiday; the millstones are garlanded and the asses that turn them are decked with wreaths and strung with loaves. — Ovid, Fasti 6.311–318 (Tier D).
  • June 15: Q.St.D.F. — quando stercus delatum fas ("when the filth has been carried out, it is lawful") — the day the sweepings of the temple are carried to the Tiber, after which ordinary business, including marriage, resumes.

M: this abbreviation is carved on the surviving Roman calendars against June 15, which makes it Tier A corroboration of Ovid's account — one of the cleanest cases in this document where an inscribed document confirms a poet.

SATURNALIA — December 17, extended in practice to the 23rd#

  • The sacrifice at the Temple of Saturn is performed Graeco ritu (Greek rite) — head uncovered — and the woollen bonds that bind the statue's feet all year are loosed for the festival. — Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.8.2–5 (Tier C); Festus s.v. Saturno (Tier C).
  • The cry: Io Saturnalia!
  • Masters serve slaves at table; a Saturnalicius princeps (mock king of the feast) is chosen by lot; the pilleus (freedman's felt cap) is worn; the toga is exchanged for the synthesis (loose dinner dress).

"...the whole crowd has given itself over to pleasure... it is not permitted to transact business."

— Seneca, Epistles 18.1 (Tier B, compressed rendering), complaining that December is now the whole month.

  • Gifts: cerei (wax tapers) and sigillaria (small clay or wax figurines). Macrobius, Sat. 1.11.49 (Tier C) explains these as substitutes for older offerings. Martial, Epigrams 14 (the Apophoreta) is a book of 223 two-line tags written to accompany Saturnalia presents — a complete surviving gift-list (Tier B).
  • The temple's principal account: Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.7–11 (Tier C), the dedicated treatment.

M: the Temple of Saturn stands in the Forum (surviving columns and architrave from the late rebuild); the sigillaria survive as cheap terracottas; and the Calendar of Philocalus (354 CE), an illustrated calendar, still shows December's festivities.

8.2 GREEK — in Attic calendar order#

PANATHENAIA — Hekatombaion 28 (month 1; midsummer)#

  • The peplos (robe) for Athena, woven by the ergastinai (working girls) and begun at the Chalkeia, carried in procession — rigged as a sail on a wheeled ship in the Greater Panathenaia.

"...shall I stand at the loom in the beautiful robe of Athena, yoking horses on the saffron cloth in its many threads?"

— Euripides, Hecuba 468–474 (Tier B, my rendering — Trojan captives imagining their future as weavers of the peplos). This is the best contemporary evidence for both the object and the woven subject (the Gigantomachy, with Athena's chariot).

  • Procession, hecatomb at the Great Altar, and distribution of meat by deme:

"...the sacrificers shall distribute the meat to the Athenian people in the Kerameikos, as at the other distributions, allotting a portion to each deme in proportion to the number of processioners each provides."

— IG II² 334, the Athenian decree regulating the Lesser Panathenaia (Tier A, my rendering). An actual administrative text for the running of a festival — how the animals are bought, who kills them, and how the meat is divided.

  • Thucydides 6.56–58 (Tier B) uses the procession as the setting for the killing of Hipparchus, and in passing records that participants formerly carried arms, and that the rejection of Harmodius' sister as kanēphoros (basket-bearer) was the provoking insult — i.e. the offices were a matter of public honour.

M: the Parthenon frieze is the largest single piece of evidence for any Greek procession — the cavalry, the chariots, the hydriaphoroi (water-carriers), the skaphephoroi (tray-bearers), the sacrificial cattle and sheep, the kanēphoroi, and the folding of the peplos at the east end. Also: the Panathenaic prize amphorae, inscribed "from the games at Athens," filled with sacred oil, of which hundreds survive — dated by the archon's name on later examples, which incidentally gives us a chronology.

ELEUSINIAN MYSTERIES — Boedromion 15–23 (month 3; early autumn)#

The core is genuinely unrecoverable; the framework is not.

The prescriptive myth:

"She refused the red wine, and bade them mix barley-meal and water with soft pennyroyal and give it to her to drink."

— Homeric Hymn to Demeter 208–210 (Tier B, my rendering) — the kykeon (κυκεών, the mixed drink). The Hymn also gives the veiled fasting, the sitting in silence on a fleece-covered stool (192–201), and the closing warning:

"...awesome rites, which one may not in any way transgress, nor inquire into, nor speak of; for great reverence of the gods restrains the voice."

— Homeric Hymn to Demeter 478–479 (Tier B, my rendering).

The sequence of the nine days (assembled from Tier B/C):

  1. Agyrmos (gathering) and the proclamation excluding the impure and those who cannot speak Greek.
  2. Boedromion 16: Halade mystai! — "To the sea, initiates!" Each bathes carrying a piglet, which is later sacrificed.
  3. Sacrifices at Athens.
  4. Boedromion 19: the procession along the Sacred Way to Eleusis, with the Iakchos cry, the kernos (multi-cup vessel) carried on the head, and the ritual abuse at the bridge over the Kephisos (gephyrismos).
  5. Pannychis (all-night celebration) at Eleusis.
  6. The telete (initiation) in the Telesterion; then the epopteia (higher grade) the following year.

"Iakchos, dwelling here in honoured shrines, Iakchos, O Iakchos, come to this meadow to dance... shaking the torch in your hand."

— Aristophanes, Frogs 323–340 (Tier B, condensed rendering) — the initiates' chorus, with the torches, the myrtle wreaths, the exclusion formula (354–371), and the whole shape of the procession, staged in the underworld.

The password, from a hostile witness:

ἐνήστευσα, ἔπιον τὸν κυκεῶνα, ἔλαβον ἐκ κίστης, ἐργασάμενος ἀπεθέμην εἰς κάλαθον καὶ ἐκ καλάθου εἰς κίστην. "I fasted; I drank the kykeon; I took from the chest; having worked, I put back into the basket, and from the basket into the chest."

— Clement of Alexandria, Protrepticus 2.21.2 (Tier C, hostile Christian polemic). The only surviving statement of the initiate's synthēma (token-formula), and it comes from an enemy. Weigh accordingly.

M: the Telesterion at Eleusis is excavated — a square hall with rock-cut tiered seating on all sides around a small central structure (the Anaktoron), holding perhaps 3,000, i.e. the architecture of watching something rather than of processing past an altar. The Ninnion Tablet (a painted votive pinax from Eleusis, 4th c. BCE, National Archaeological Museum, Athens) shows the procession, the torches, the kernos carried on the head, and initiates seated on fleece-covered stools — the Hymn's own detail, in paint.

PYANOPSIA — Pyanopsion 7 (month 4; autumn)#

Two components, both with object and words surviving.

  1. The pyanos — a porridge of every kind of pulse and grain boiled together and offered to Apollo.
  2. The eiresiōnē (εἰρεσιώνη) — an olive or laurel branch wound with wool and hung with fruits, loaves, and small flasks of oil, honey, and wine, carried by a boy with both parents living, and fixed above the house door for the year, replaced annually.

"The eiresione brings figs and rich loaves, and honey in a pot, and olive oil to rub down with, and a cup of strong wine, so that she may drink and go to sleep."

— the eiresione song, quoted at Plutarch, Theseus 22.6–7 (Tier C, my rendering). Also Aristophanes, Knights 728–729 and Plutus 1054 with scholia (Tier B/C); Suda s.v. εἰρεσιώνη (Tier C).

This is one of very few Greek rites for which we have the object, the words, and the annual cadence together — which makes it unusually safe to reconstruct.

THESMOPHORIA — Pyanopsion 11–13 (month 4; autumn)#

Women only; three days — Anodos/Kathodos (ascent/descent), Nesteia (fasting), Kalligeneia (fair offspring).

The procedural text is a scholion, and it is indispensable:

"They throw living piglets down into the chambers... and the women called Antlētriai (bailers), having kept themselves pure for three days, go down into the megara and bring up the rotted remains, and place them on the altars; and they believe that whoever takes of this and scatters it with the seed will have a good harvest. They say there are snakes below around the chasms, which eat most of what is thrown down — and therefore they make a clatter when the women draw up the remains, so that the snakes will withdraw."

— Scholion to Lucian, Dialogues of the Courtesans 2.1 (Tier C, my rendering). Also thrown down: pine branches, and dough models of snakes and of male genitals.

The assembly and the fasting: Aristophanes, Thesmophoriazusae (Tier B) — the entire play — gives the herald's prayer (295–371), the assembly procedure copied from the men's, and the women's oath. The Nesteia is passed sitting on the ground on mats of lygos (λύγος, agnus castus): Pliny, NH 24.59 (Tier B) says Athenian matrons strew their beds with it during the Thesmophoria for chastity.

The legal weight: Isaeus 3.80 (Tier A) — a man's paying for the Thesmophoria feast for the deme's women on his wife's behalf is submitted as proof that a marriage existed.

⚠️ The Thesmophoria is celebrated in many cities at different times of year. Pyanopsion is the Attic placement only. Do not generalize the date.

M: terracotta piglet votives are recovered in enormous numbers from Demeter sanctuaries throughout the Greek world; sunken pits identified as megara are excavated at Demeter sanctuaries including Priene and in Sicily. The piglet is one of the best-attested cult objects in Greek archaeology.

RURAL DIONYSIA — Poseideon (month 6; midwinter)#

A complete village-scale procession, staged on-stage in order:

"Keep holy silence! Let the basket-bearer come forward a little... Set the basket down, daughter, so we may make the offering. Wife, hand me the ladle, so I can pour the broth over the flat-cake. Good — now you, Xanthias, you must stand the phallus-pole upright behind the basket-bearer, and I'll follow and sing the phallic song."

— Aristophanes, Acharnians 241–262 (Tier B, condensed rendering).

Order of persons and objects: silence — kanēphoros (basket-bearer, here the daughter) — basket set down — libation of broth over the cake — phallus-pole raised behind her by the slave — the householder follows singing to Phales. His wife watches from the roof.

M: this exact configuration (basket-bearer in front, phallus-pole behind, singer following) is the standard Dionysiac procession scene on Attic vases. The comedy and the pottery match.

ANTHESTERIA — Anthesterion 11–13 (month 8; late winter)#

"...the older Dionysia, celebrated on the twelfth of the month Anthesterion."

— Thucydides 2.15.4 (Tier B, compressed), establishing it as the oldest festival of Dionysus at Athens.

Day 1 — Pithoigia (Πιθοίγια, jar-opening). The new wine jars are opened and a libation poured from the jar before anyone drinks. — Plutarch, Moralia 655e (Tier C).

Day 2 — Choes (Χόες, wine-jugs). Sanctuaries are roped off; the day is miara (μιαρά, polluted). Each drinker has his own table, his own jug, and his own garland, and they drink in silence at a trumpet signal, as a competition.

"He was not turned away, but was made welcome as a guest — though he had a separate table, alone, as he was the son of Agamemnon; and they set before each man his own measure of wine in a separate vessel, and they kept silence... the festival is still kept, and the people of Athens still call it the Feast of Pitchers."

— Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris 947–960 (Tier B, condensed rendering). Euripides is explaining the rite by the polluted Orestes, and — critically — says the custom persists in his own day.

On the same day, the Basilinna (wife of the archon basileus) is given in marriage to Dionysus at the Boukoleion, and administers the oath to the fourteen Gerarai at the altars. The oath is read out in a criminal trial:

"I keep myself holy and am pure and undefiled from all else that is unclean, and from intercourse with a man, and I will keep the Theoinia and the Iobaccheia to Dionysus according to ancestral custom and at the appointed times."

— [Demosthenes] 59.78 (Against Neaera) (Tier A, my rendering), with the inscribed pillar in the sanctuary cited in court as corroboration. This is the best-documented Greek priestly ritual in existence, because it was evidence in a prosecution.

Day 3 — Chytroi (Χύτροι, pots). A panspermia (πανσπερμία, all-seeds) — a pot of every kind of grain and pulse, boiled — is offered to Hermes Chthonios on behalf of the dead, and no one eats of it. Buckthorn is chewed and the doorways smeared with pitch against the spirits. At the close, the cry:

θύραζε, Κᾶρες, οὐκέτ' Ἀνθεστήρια. "Out the door, Spirits — the Anthesteria is over."

— Scholia to Aristophanes, Frogs 218 and Acharnians 1076; Suda s.v. θύραζε Κᾶρες; Photius s.v. μιαρὰ ἡμέρα (all Tier C). ⚠️ Ancient sources are split on whether the word is Kēres (death-spirits) or Kares (Carians, i.e. slaves). The scholiasts argue about it. Leave the ambiguity standing.

M: miniature choes (small wine-jugs) are found in the graves of Athenian children, decorated with toddlers crawling, playing, and pulling small carts. The literary tradition says three-year-olds received their first small chous at this festival; the children's graves contain exactly those jugs. This is the single most satisfying text-and-object correspondence in Greek religion, and it is why the children's rite is regarded as certain.

DIASIA — Anthesterion 23 (month 8)#

"...the greatest festival of Zeus Meilichios, called the Diasia, held outside the city, at which the whole people sacrifice — many offering not animal victims but local offerings."

— Thucydides 1.126.6 (Tier B, my rendering). The scholia and Lucian's scholiast (Tier C) identify those "local offerings" as popana (πόπανα) — cakes moulded in the shape of animals, for those who could not afford a beast. Aristophanes, Clouds 408–411 and 864 (Tier B) jokes about the family's Diasia sausage and the toy cart bought at the festival.

The Diasia is the best ancient evidence that a bloodless substitute was accepted at a major public festival — directly relevant to any modern domestic practice.

M: the Piraeus reliefs of Zeus Meilichios, 4th c. BCE, inscribed with the god's name and showing a colossal coiled bearded serpent, are the iconography of this cult. Some show worshippers approaching the snake with offerings at a small altar.

8.3 NON-ATTIC GREEK#

KARNEIA (Sparta and the Dorian cities) — the month Karneios (late summer)#

The clearest example of a major festival with no Athenian counterpart and procedure recorded for one city only.

"...the Spartans said that they could not march out and break their law, for it was the ninth day of the month, and they would not go out on the ninth when the moon was not full."

— Herodotus 6.106 (Tier B, condensed rendering) — the Karneia delayed Sparta before Marathon; Herodotus 7.206 records the same before Thermopylae. The festival's calendar constraint changed the course of two wars, which is the strongest possible evidence that it was real and binding.

"The Karneia is an imitation of military training. There are nine places set up, similar to tents, and in each of them nine men dine; everything is done at the command of a herald. Each place holds three phratries, and the Karneia lasts nine days."

— Demetrius of Scepsis, quoted at Athenaeus 4.141e–f (Tier C, my rendering).

Also attested: the Karneatai (unmarried men chosen to serve for four years), the staphylodromoi ("grape-runners") who pursue a garlanded runner, whose capture is a good omen for the city. — Hesychius s.v. σταφυλοδρόμοι (Tier C); Pausanias 3.13.3–5 (Tier B) on the Spartan cult of Apollo Karneios and its priesthood.

9. WHERE THE SOURCES CONFLICT, AND WHY#

Classifying a conflict matters more than resolving it. Five distinct causes.

9.1 Genuine local difference — both sources are correct#

9.2 Two-rite systems within one culture — both correct, applied to different gods#

  • Roman head-covering. Standard sacrifice is capite velato (Vergil, Aen. 3.405–407); Saturn and Hercules at the Ara Maxima receive sacrifice capite aperto, Graeco ritu (Macrobius, Sat. 1.8.2, 3.6.17; Servius, ad Aen. 3.407). The Romans named the two rites themselves.
  • Olympian vs. chthonian (§3.7). Nearly every apparent Greek contradiction about altar height, victim colour, time of day, and whether the meat is eaten resolves onto this axis.
  • Bloodless offering as a recognized class. The Genius receives no blood (Censorinus 2.2–3; Tibullus 2.2); the Diasia accepts dough animals (Thucydides 1.126.6); Horace's Phidyle placates the Penates with spelt and salt (Odes 3.23.19–20); the Parentalia asks for a tile, grain, salt, wine-soaked bread, and violets (Ovid, Fasti 2.535–539). Not exceptions — nēphalia, popana, thylēmata, far et mica are categories.

9.3 Scope and status differences — who is obliged, versus who is devout#

The most important case in this document: Cato 143 vs. Plautus, Aulularia. Cato says Kalends, Nones, Ides, and feast days. Plautus' Lar says cottidie — every day. These are nearly contemporary (c. 195 and c. 160 BCE) and both are good sources.

The resolution is scope, not date. Cato is stating the minimum obligation of a farm overseer's wife, in a chapter that immediately restricts her from performing any rite at all without orders. Plautus is describing the voluntary devotion of a daughter of the house whom the god singles out as unusually attentive — the prologue's entire point is that she is exceptional and that the god rewards her for it. Cato gives the floor; Plautus gives the ceiling. Both are true, and together they define the range.

The same distinction governs offering scale: Horace, Odes 3.23 exists to say that the poor woman's salt-grain is as effective as the rich man's victim. Roman household cult had an explicit doctrine of proportionality — pro copia, "according to her means," in Cato's own words at 143.

9.4 Change over time — both correct, in different centuries#

  • Roman funerals were originally nocturnal, which is why torches were carried in daylight processions — Servius, ad Aen. 11.143 (Tier C).
  • Confarreatio became so rare that Augustus and Tiberius had to legislate to find eligible candidates for the flaminate — Tacitus, Annals 4.16 (Tier B).
  • Sumptuary law is itself evidence of change: laws forbid what people are actually doing. Every restriction in Solon's funeral law and in the Twelve Tables is a description of contemporary behaviour, inverted.
  • M: Greek burial practice alternates between inhumation and cremation by region and period. Homer's exclusively cremating world is not the world of the classical Kerameikos, where both occur.

9.5 The ancient author did not know — and says so#

The largest category, and the most useful to internalize.

  • Plutarch, Roman Questions is 113 questions about Roman rites; he usually offers two to five explanations and declines to choose. RQ 87 (the hasta caelibaris), RQ 29 (the threshold), RQ 2 (five torches), RQ 68 (the Lupercal dog), RQ 30 (Gaius/Gaia) are all cases where the act was certain and the meaning was already lost.
  • Livy 1.9.12 and Plutarch, Romulus 15 give incompatible explanations of Talassio; Plutarch lists five.
  • Ovid, Fasti routinely gives alternative aitia and lets them stand (e.g. Lemuria from Remuria, 5.451–484). Ovid's procedure is reliable; Ovid's etymology and origin are literary. Treat these as two different grades of evidence inside one author.
  • Varro was already reconstructing archaic practice antiquarianly in the 1st c. BCE; Verrius Flaccus used Varro; Festus epitomized Verrius; Paulus the Deacon epitomized Festus. Much of what we call "a Roman source" is a summary of a summary of a reconstruction. Say so when citing.

Working rule: when two sources conflict, ask in order — local? two-rite? scope? period? or did the author simply not know? Only if none of the five applies is it an error.

10. HONEST GAPS — do not let anyone fill these for you#

  1. No daily prayer text to the Lares exists. Cato 143 gives the cadence and the act; the words are not recorded, and Cato gives words freely elsewhere, so the silence is meaningful. Any "traditional Roman daily prayer" in circulation is a modern composition.
  2. No Roman evening rite is attested. The cena offering (§4.4) is the evening observance because the cena was the evening meal. Lamps burned at lararia — the archaeology shows it — but no text records words said over them.
  3. No continuous account of an ordinary person's day of worship survives in either language. §3.2–3.5 and §4.3–4.6 are assembled from fragments and should always be presented as assemblies.
  4. The interior of the Eleusinian telete is lost. What survives is a hostile Christian paraphrase (Clement) and the architecture. It cannot be recovered.
  5. Greek household cult is reconstructed almost entirely from Athens, and mostly from comedy and forensic oratory. Extending it to other poleis without local evidence is unsound.
  6. The Roman tollere liberum is an idiom, not an attested ceremony (§5.2 Step 4).
  7. Fixed monthly deity-days at Athens (§3.5) are a modern composite from scattered evidence, not a single ancient list.
  8. Lararium practice is known textually from two sentences and materially from five hundred shrines. Cato 143 and the Aulularia prologue are the only textual anchors. Everything about how a lararium looked — the Genius veiled with patera, the two dancing Lares with rhyton and situla, the agathodaemon serpent at an altar with egg and pinecone, the kitchen placement, the imported statuettes — is Tier M only. That is a perfectly sound evidentiary basis, but it must be labelled as archaeological, not textual, or it will be challenged.
  9. Sparta is described only by outsiders, most writing after its collapse, when "Lycurgan Sparta" had become an ideological construct. Only the Karneia (§8.3) and the mourning restrictions (§9.1) are attested well enough to use. The reported Spartan marriage customs are too thinly and too late attested to reconstruct, and are excluded from this document.
  10. Where a date is not attested, it is not attested. The Compitalia and Ambarvalia are movable; the Amphidromia is fifth or seventh day; the Anthesteria cry is Kēres or Kares. Record the range, not a choice.

11.3 Inscriptions and documentary texts [M]#

  • The Cyrene cathartic law — SEG IX 72 / LSS 115, 4th c. BCE. Birth pollution, its radius, and purification. [M]
  • IG XII 5, 593 — the sacred law of Iulis, Keos, 5th c. BCE. The fullest Greek funerary procedure: shrouds, silence, sea-water purification, no thirtieth-day rite. [M]
  • LSAM 16 — the law of Gambreion, 3rd c. BCE. Mourning lengths by sex; the officer who enforces them. [M]
  • IG II² 334 — the Lesser Panathenaia decree. Purchase of victims, the procession, meat distribution by deme. [M]
  • IG II² 1237 — the decrees of the Demotionidai, 396/5 BCE. Phratry scrutiny, oath, and penalties. [M]
  • CID I 9 — the Labyadai law, Delphi. Offerings and funerals regulated for a single phratry. [M]
  • CIL VI 1527 — the Laudatio Turiae, late 1st c. BCE. An actual surviving funeral eulogy, inscribed at length. [M]
  • Altars inscribed SEI DEO SEI DEIVAE SAC ("sacred, whether to a god or goddess"), Rome. The Catonian formula in stone. [M]
  • Brauron temple inventories. Itemized lists of women's garments dedicated to Artemis. [M]
  • Fasti Antiates Maiores (Antium) — the only surviving pre-Julian Roman calendar, painted. [M]
  • Fasti Praenestini (Praeneste) — Verrius Flaccus' own calendar, with his commentary carved beside the days. [M]
  • The Calendar of Philocalus, 354 CE — illustrated calendar of the later Empire. [M]
  • Tomb foundation inscriptions endowing Parentalia and Rosalia observances with named sums and dates. [M]

11.4 Sites, monuments, and object classes [M]#

Greek household and cult:

  • Olynthos (destroyed 348 BCE) and Priene — excavated ordinary houses: fixed hearths, courtyard altar bases, in-house deposits.
  • Athenian Agora and Delos — hekataia (triple Hekate statuettes) and herms recovered at doorways and street corners.
  • Perirrhantēria — stone lustral basins in situ at sanctuary entrances (Isthmia, Olympia).
  • Piraeus reliefs of Zeus Meilichios, 4th c. BCE — inscribed, showing a colossal bearded serpent approached by worshippers at an altar. Directly parallel to the Roman lararium agathodaemon.
  • Attic red- and black-figure sacrifice scenes — kanoun, chernips, machaira, fillets, spitted splanchna, the tail on the altar flame.

Greek life-cycle and festival:

  • Brauron — mass deposits of girls' pre-marriage dedications.
  • Loutrophoros, lebes gamikos, wedding pyxides — the vessel shapes of the wedding, and the loutrophoros reused as a marker on the graves of the unmarried.
  • Dipylon kraters and amphorae, Kerameikos (8th c. BCE); black-figure funerary pinakes (6th c.); white-ground lekythoi (5th c.) — four centuries of prothesis imagery.
  • Children's graves containing miniature choes — the Anthesteria rite confirmed by burial assemblage.
  • Terracotta piglet votives from Demeter sanctuaries across the Greek world; megara pits at Priene and in Sicily.
  • Eleusis: the Telesterion (tiered hall around the Anaktoron) and the Ninnion Tablet (painted pinax: procession, torches, kernos, fleece-covered stool).
  • The Parthenon frieze and the Panathenaic prize amphorae.

Roman household:

  • Pompeii and Herculaneum lararia — c. 500 recorded shrines; the aedicula, niche, and painted-panel forms; predominantly kitchen placement; the Genius capite velato with patera and acerra, two Lares with rhyton and situla, agathodaemon serpent(s) at an altar with egg and pinecone; excavated contents including lamps, coins, bronze statuettes (Roman and imported deities), incense burners, eggshell, and burnt residue. House of the Vettii (VI.15.1) is the type-specimen aedicula.
  • Delos — painted compital shrines with ministri at the altar; Pompeii street-corner shrines; the Belvedere Altar and the Augustan vicomagistri altars, Rome.

Roman sacrifice and death:

  • Altar of Domitius Ahenobarbus (Louvre) — the suovetaurilia with victimarii, tibicen, and veiled officiant at a portable altar.
  • Ara Pacis processional frieze; Trajan's Column sacrifice scenes; the Boscoreale cups; sacrificial implements (patera, simpulum, acerra, lituus, aspergillum) as a standing set on coin reverses and altar reliefs.
  • Tomb of the Haterii relief (Vatican) — the collocatio: draped couch, corner torches, garlands, mourners, flute-players.
  • Amiternum relief (Aquila) — the pompa funebris: canopied bier on a platform, tibicines and cornicines, praeficae, family.
  • Isola Sacra necropolis (Portus) and the Vatican necropolis — tomb dining facilities and profusio pipes running from the surface into the burial for pouring offerings.
  • Gold and leather bullae; Saturnalia sigillaria; inscribed termini and cippi; dextrarum iunctio marriage sarcophagi; the Temple of Saturn in the Forum.

11.5 Standard modern reference tools#

These are collections and indexes, not authorities — use them to find the primary text, then cite the primary text.

  • Inscriptiones Graecae (IG); Supplementum Epigraphicum Graecum (SEG); Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum (CIL).
  • Sokolowski, Lois sacrées des cités grecques (LSCG), …de l'Asie Mineure (LSAM), …Supplément (LSS) — the sacred laws, collected. The successor collection is CGRN (Collection of Greek Ritual Norms), which is open-access and gives text with translation.
  • Thesaurus Cultus et Rituum Antiquorum (ThesCRA) — the standard catalogue of ritual acts, personnel, and objects, indexed to both texts and monuments. The fastest route from "what did this look like" to the evidence.
  • Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae (LIMC) — the catalogue of divine iconography.
  • Roscher, Ausführliches Lexikon and RE (Pauly–Wissowa) — older, exhaustive, still the fullest source-collections for minor deities and obscure rites.
  • Degrassi, Inscriptiones Italiae XIII.2 — the Roman calendars (Fasti), assembled with commentary. The indispensable volume for calendar work: every surviving fragment of every Roman calendar, aligned day by day.
  • Pompeii in Pictures — photographic documentation of Pompeian structures by address, including household shrines.
