How this calendar is reckoned

The frame is the ordinary Gregorian year[cite: 1]. Two different kinds of festival are laid over it[cite: 1].

The Roman year

Roman festivals sit on fixed days, on the same numbered day as in the Republican and Julian fasti: the Parilia on 21 April, the Saturnalia opening on 17 December[cite: 1]. Each day also carries its inscriptional form — a.d. XI Kal. Sept. — counted from the Kalends, Nones and Ides of the Gregorian month[cite: 1]. A handful of Roman feasts were feriae conceptivae, announced each year by a magistrate rather than fixed: the Compitalia, the Sementivae, the Ludi[cite: 1]. These are placed on their conventional modern dates and flagged as set by convention[cite: 1].

The Greek year

Every Greek city ran a lunar month against a solar year[cite: 1]. A month begins at the noumenia, the first sighting of the new crescent, and runs 29 or 30 days until the next[cite: 1]. The year begins with the first new moon after a fixed solar point — the summer solstice at Athens, the autumn equinox in Macedon, the winter solstice in Boeotia[cite: 1]. Because twelve lunations fall about eleven days short of the solar year, roughly every third year carries a thirteenth month; this page inserts it as a doubled month (a Second Poseideon at Athens) and does not repeat that month's festivals[cite: 1].

New moons and solstices are computed from the periodic series in Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, and are good to a few minutes[cite: 1]. The conjunction itself is invisible, so the first day of the month is set one day after it by default; ancient practice varied and the offset can be changed under Display[cite: 1]. Shifting it moves every Greek date on the page by that many days[cite: 1].

Games that do not fall every year

The Olympia, the Pythia, the Isthmia, the Nemea and a few others ran on cycles of two, four, seven or eight years[cite: 1]. They appear only in the years they are due, and the day panel says so[cite: 1]. The cycles are pinned to the modern convention that the Olympic year is divisible by four, with the Pythia in the third year of each Olympiad; if your tradition counts the Olympiads differently, change the anchor year in the entry rather than the interval[cite: 1].

What the flags mean

A tradition marked medium has a well-attested month list whose order or year-start is reconstructed[cite: 1]. low means the month order itself is conjectural; individual festival days in these systems are usually modern conventions placed in the right season[cite: 1]. placeholder means the month names have not been entered at all and the system is scaffolding awaiting the epigraphic list[cite: 1]. Where only the month or the season of a festival is known, the entry is marked day fixed by convention in the day panel, and those entries can be hidden entirely[cite: 1].

The point of the flags is that this calendar should never quietly present a guess as a fact[cite: 1]. Where the evidence is thin, celebrate in the right season and say so[cite: 1].

Extending the calendar to a new year

Nothing on this page is a lookup table[cite: 1]. Moon phases, month lengths, intercalation and every Greek festival date are computed in the browser, so there is no annual data file to fetch and no yearly update to publish[cite: 1]. The calendar already runs to December 3000[cite: 1]. Type a year into the box beside the month arrows to go there[cite: 1].

1. How the range works

Near the top of the script block[cite: 1]:

var CFG = {
  yearStart: 2026,   /* first navigable year */
  yearEnd:   3000,   /* last navigable year  */

Holding a thousand years of seventeen lunar calendars in memory at once would cost hundreds of megabytes, so the page computes a three-year window around whatever you are looking at and rebuilds it — in a few milliseconds — when you walk off the edge[cite: 1]. Widening yearEnd therefore costs nothing at load time[cite: 1]. The underlying algorithms are published for roughly 1000–3000 CE; beyond that the periodic series drift and the precession terms in the season calculation stop being trustworthy, which is why the ceiling sits where it does[cite: 1].

One caveat worth stating plainly: this is astronomy, not history[cite: 1]. A date in 2400 is a correct prediction of where the new moon will fall, not a claim that anyone will be keeping the Karneia then[cite: 1].

2. Spot-check the astronomy

Before publishing a new range, check three or four new moon dates against an ephemeris (the US Naval Observatory or HM Nautical Almanac both publish tables)[cite: 1]. Open a day that is marked as a new lunar month and confirm the conjunction falls where you expect[cite: 1]. An error here would shift a whole system's year, not a single day[cite: 1].

3. Watch for the thirteenth month

Open the day panel on any date and each enabled system reports its month as Hekatombaion 12 — month 1 of 12[cite: 1]. In an intercalary year it will read month 7 of 13 and the doubled month is named[cite: 1]. Confirm that the doubled month falls where your tradition expects it[cite: 1]. The insertion point is the interc field on each system, a zero-based index into its month list — 5 doubles the sixth month[cite: 1].

4. Adding or correcting a festival

Roman entries live in the ROMAN array and use the Gregorian month and day[cite: 1]:

{ m:4, d:21, n:'Parilia', g:['pales'], span:1,
  note:'Text shown in the day panel.' }

Greek entries live in the fest array of their system and use the lunar month number and the day within it[cite: 1]:

{ mi:1, d:12, n:'Kronia', g:['saturn'], span:1,
  approx:false, note:'...' }

Add cycle:[4,2028] to a festival that is not annual: the first number is the interval in years, the second is any year in which it falls[cite: 1]. The engine tests the year in which that lunar year began, so a festival in a month that lands in January still belongs to the previous anchor year[cite: 1].

mi is the position in that system's months list, counting from 1[cite: 1]. d is the day of the lunar month, where 1 is the noumenia[cite: 1]. Set approx:true whenever you are placing a festival by season rather than by attestation — that is what keeps the flag honest[cite: 1]. Every key in g must exist in DEITIES, or the god filter will not find it; add new gods there first[cite: 1].

5. Filling in a placeholder system

The two Opuntian Locrian systems ship as numbered scaffolding[cite: 1]. Replace the twelve strings in months with the epigraphic names, set conf to 'low' or 'medium', rewrite src to say what the reconstruction rests on, and add festivals[cite: 1]. Until then they are best left switched off[cite: 1].

6. Publishing

The page is a single file with no dependencies[cite: 1]. Commit it as index.html in the repository or in a /docs folder, enable Pages for that branch, and it is live[cite: 1]. Because everything is computed, an old cached copy is still correct for its own range — readers only need to reload to see a corrected one[cite: 1].

Sources and cautions

The Roman entries follow the surviving fasti and Ovid's Fasti, which covers January to June in detail and leaves the second half of the year to the calendar inscriptions, Varro, Festus and Macrobius[cite: 1]. Where Ovid gives a story for a day, the note here summarises it in a sentence; the poem itself is worth reading beside the calendar rather than through it[cite: 1].

The Athenian festival year is the best documented in the Greek world and its entries can be trusted to the day[cite: 1]. Delphi, Macedon, Rhodes, Thessaly, Boeotia and Epidauros have solid month lists and thinner festival evidence[cite: 1]. Sparta, Argos, Elis, Crete, Aetolia, Corinth and the Sicilian cities are reconstructions: the festivals are real and their seasons are known, but the day within the month is usually a modern choice[cite: 1]. Ozolian Locris genuinely numbered its months rather than naming them[cite: 1].

Where a rite was restricted in antiquity — the Thesmophoria and the Bona Dea to women, the Mysteries to initiates — the entry records that restriction as a matter of history[cite: 1]. What a modern group does with it is a decision for the group, not for a calendar[cite: 1].

A system can look almost empty in a given year without being broken: Elis holds little but the Olympia and its attendant rites, and those fall only every fourth year[cite: 1]. Step forward a year or two and it fills[cite: 1].

Corrections are welcome[cite: 1]. A festival moved by good evidence is worth more than a full page[cite: 1].
